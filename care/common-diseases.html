Common Diseases

By Pet Zone SD Team, September 2026

The Short Version
  • Most apisto health problems trace back to water quality, not bad luck
  • Quarantine every new fish for 2–4 weeks before adding to an established tank
  • Catch problems early — apistos hide illness longer than hardier community fish
  • A clean, well-cycled, low-stress tank prevents far more than any medication cures

Prevention Beats Treatment

The honest truth about apisto health: the overwhelming majority of disease outbreaks in this genus trace back to water quality, stress, or an unquarantined new arrival — not bad luck. A stable, well-cycled tank with clean water and low stress prevents far more problems than any medicine cabinet full of treatments cures after the fact. Our water quality guide and water parameters guide are, in a real sense, the first two pages of this one.

Reading Early Warning Signs

Apistos tend to hide illness longer than hardier community fish, so subtle behavior changes matter more than they might with a livebearer or a barb. Watch for:

  • Clamped fins, held close to the body instead of relaxed and spread
  • Reduced appetite or ignoring food entirely
  • Hiding more than usual, especially a normally out-going fish suddenly staying tucked away
  • Labored or rapid gill movement
  • Color fading or a washed-out look, distinct from normal stress-coloring
  • Visible spots, patches, fuzzy growths, or fin damage

Catching any of these early and reacting quickly — usually starting with a water test and a water change — resolves far more cases than waiting to see if it clears up on its own.

Ich (White Spot Disease)

Small white spots across the body and fins, often with flashing (scraping against decor) as an early sign before spots are visible. A common parasite that spreads quickly in a shared tank. Raising temperature gradually to speed the parasite's life cycle, combined with a proper ich medication and consistent water changes, is the standard treatment. Because it spreads fast, treat the whole tank, not just the visibly affected fish.

Fungal Infections

Cotton-like white or gray growths, usually appearing on a fish already weakened by injury, poor water quality, or stress. Fungus is typically secondary — something else compromised the fish first. Treat with an appropriate antifungal medication and, just as importantly, identify and fix whatever underlying issue let it take hold.

Bacterial Infections

Fin rot (ragged, receding fin edges, sometimes with a reddish tinge), and less commonly more serious internal bacterial infections showing as bloating, redness, or open sores. Fin rot in particular is very often a water quality problem before it's an infection problem — check ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate before reaching for medication, since clean water alone resolves mild cases.

Hole-in-the-Head and Similar Erosion

Pitting or erosion around the head and lateral line, more associated with larger cichlids but occasionally seen in this genus. Linked to water quality, nutritional gaps, and in some cases internal parasites. Improve water quality first, add dietary variety, and consult a vet or experienced breeder if it progresses rather than improves.

Internal Parasites

Stringy white feces, a hollowed or sunken-belly look despite normal eating, or slow wasting despite good appetite can point to internal parasites, particularly in wild-caught imports. This is one of the strongest arguments for quarantine — treating suspected internal parasites (with an appropriate dewormer) during quarantine, before a new fish ever reaches your main tank, keeps a problem from becoming a tank-wide one.

Quarantine: The Single Best Prevention Tool

Every new fish — species-guide-worthy show fish or a simple restock — should spend two to four weeks in a separate quarantine tank before joining an established display. This does three things: gives you time to observe for disease before it can spread, lets a stressed fish settle without the added pressure of established tankmates, and gives you a controlled space to treat anything that does show up. It's genuinely the highest-leverage habit in this hobby for avoiding outbreaks.

When to Ask for Help

If a problem isn't responding to water changes and an appropriate first-line treatment within a few days, or you're not confident in the diagnosis, don't keep guessing — reach out. Pet Zone SD and the wider hobby community (see our community page) are both good resources for a second opinion before a treatable problem becomes a fatal one.
