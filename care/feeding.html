Feeding

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Short Version
  • Omnivores — quality dry food as a base, live/frozen for color and conditioning
  • Small portions, 2–3 times daily
  • Live or frozen foods are close to essential for breeding condition
  • Feed what clears in about two minutes — overfeeding fouls water fast in a small tank

What Apistos Eat in the Wild

Every species in this genus is an omnivore, feeding on insect larvae, small crustaceans, worms, and other invertebrates picked out of leaf litter and soft substrate, with some incidental plant matter along the way. That background is why a varied diet — not a single food type — produces the healthiest, best-colored fish in the aquarium.

A Base Diet

A quality small-pellet or flake food formulated for cichlids or general community fish is a fine daily staple. Look for something sized appropriately for a small mouth — apistos are not large fish, and a pellet sized for oscars or angelfish will mostly go untouched. Rotate between a couple of quality brands rather than relying on one food exclusively; small formulation differences add up to better overall nutrition.

Live and Frozen Foods

This is where color, vigor, and breeding readiness really come from. Live or frozen foods aren't strictly mandatory for basic survival, but they make a visible difference and are close to essential if you want fish in breeding condition:

  • Bloodworms (frozen or live) — a staple most apistos take eagerly
  • Brine shrimp (frozen, live, or newly hatched for fry) — good all-around conditioning food
  • Daphnia — light, easily digested, good for regular rotation
  • Microworms and grindal worms — small enough for fry, useful as a culture you can grow at home
  • Live blackworms — a rich, high-value conditioning food, used in moderation

Frozen foods are the practical middle ground for most keepers — no live culture to maintain, minimal disease risk compared to some live foods, and nearly as effective for conditioning as true live foods.

Feeding Schedule

Small portions, two to three times a day, beat one large feeding. Apistos have small stomachs and forage more or less continuously in the wild — frequent small meals match that pattern better than a single large one, and reduce the risk of uneaten food fouling the water. A simple rule of thumb: feed what the fish clear in about two minutes, and if food is still sitting on the substrate after five, you fed too much.

Conditioning for Breeding

If you're working toward a spawn, step up live and frozen foods for two to three weeks before you expect it. This mimics the seasonal abundance that triggers breeding behavior in the wild and reliably improves both spawn size and how well a pair — especially the female — holds up through the guarding period that follows. See our breeding guide for the full picture.

Feeding Fry

Newly free-swimming fry need food small enough to fit their mouths — infusoria or a liquid fry food for the first several days, moving up to newly hatched brine shrimp and microworms as they grow. Underfed fry grow unevenly and are more vulnerable to disease; most experienced breeders feed fry more frequently, in smaller amounts, than adult fish. Species pages cover the specific fry-care timeline for each fish.

What to Avoid

  • Overfeeding. The single most common mistake — excess food breaks down into ammonia and drives exactly the water quality problems this genus is least tolerant of. See our water quality guide.
  • A single food type, indefinitely. Even a "complete" pellet benefits from variety; nutritional gaps show up over months, not days.
  • Feeder fish and unrinsed live foods from unreliable sources — a real vector for parasites and disease. See our common diseases guide.
