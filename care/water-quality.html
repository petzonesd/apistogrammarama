Water Quality

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Short Version
  • Ammonia and nitrite should always read zero in an established tank
  • Keep nitrate low — this genus is unusually intolerant of nitrate buildup
  • Weekly water changes, 25–30%, are the baseline for most apisto tanks
  • Test, don't guess — especially in a new or recently stocked tank

Water Parameters vs. Water Quality

It's worth separating two related but different ideas. Our water parameters guide covers pH, hardness, and temperature — the baseline chemistry a species wants. This page covers water quality — the ongoing maintenance job of keeping ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate in check regardless of what your baseline pH happens to be. You can have perfect pH and still lose fish to a water quality problem, and the reverse is true too.

The Nitrogen Cycle, Briefly

Fish waste, uneaten food, and decaying plant matter break down into ammonia, which is toxic even in small amounts. Beneficial bacteria that colonize your filter media and substrate convert ammonia into nitrite (also toxic), and a second set of bacteria convert nitrite into nitrate (far less toxic, but still harmful in excess). This is the nitrogen cycle, and it's the single most important biological process running in your tank at all times.

A tank needs to be fully cycled — with an established colony of both bacteria types — before it can safely hold a normal fish load. Adding apistos to an uncycled or under-cycled tank is one of the most common and most avoidable causes of early losses in this genus. See our tank setup guide for cycling basics before stocking.

Target Numbers

  • Ammonia (NH3/NH4): 0 ppm, always, in an established tank. Any detectable ammonia means something is wrong — overstocking, overfeeding, a filter problem, or an incomplete cycle.
  • Nitrite (NO2): 0 ppm, always, for the same reasons as ammonia. Nitrite is what typically spikes mid-cycle, after ammonia has started converting but before the second bacteria colony is established.
  • Nitrate (NO3): This genus is genuinely more sensitive to nitrate than a lot of community fish — keep it under 20 ppm where you can, and treat sustained readings above 40 ppm as a signal to change water more often or reduce stocking/feeding.

Why This Genus Is Less Forgiving

Apistogramma evolved in soft, often nutrient-poor blackwater environments with naturally low dissolved organics — very different from the nutrient-rich water many hardier aquarium fish tolerate without issue. That evolutionary background is a big part of why this genus shows stress and health problems at nitrate levels a livebearer or barb would shrug off. Don't assume "the water looks fine" is the same as "the water tests fine" — apistos can look outwardly okay right up until a water quality problem becomes a visible one.

Water Change Routine

A weekly 25–30% water change is a reasonable baseline for most apisto tanks, adjusted up if you're running a heavier bioload (more fish, more feeding, a breeding setup with fry) or down slightly in a lightly stocked, heavily planted tank that's handling nitrate well on its own. Consistency matters more than the exact percentage — a tank on a reliable weekly schedule outperforms one that gets a big emergency change every few months when something goes wrong.

Always match temperature and dechlorinate replacement water before adding it — a sudden temperature swing or a dose of chlorine/chloramine undoes the benefit of the water change itself.

Testing Routine

A liquid-reagent test kit (not test strips, which are notoriously imprecise) covering ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, and pH is worth owning if you keep more than a couple of tanks. Test:

  • Daily to every few days during initial cycling
  • Weekly for the first month or two after stocking a new tank
  • Periodically — every few weeks — once a tank is established and stable, or any time you notice a behavior change that might signal a water quality problem

Overfeeding: The Quiet Cause

A meaningful share of water quality problems in apisto tanks trace back to overfeeding rather than understocking filtration. Excess food that goes uneaten breaks down into exactly the ammonia and organic load this genus tolerates least well. See our feeding guide for portion sizing — when in doubt, feed less, not more.
