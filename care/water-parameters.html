Water Parameters

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Short Version
  • pH: 5.0–7.0 for most species, with true blackwater specialists wanting the low end
  • Hardness: Soft, generally under 10 dGH
  • Temperature: 73–84°F (23–29°C) depending on species
  • TDS: Most apistos do best under 150 ppm; domestic-bred lines tolerate more
  • Stability matters more than chasing a perfect number

Why Water Chemistry Matters More Here Than With Most Fish

Apistogramma comes almost entirely from soft, acidic Amazonian and Orinoco drainages — blackwater and clearwater creeks with little mineral content and a naturally low pH from decaying leaf litter and tannins. That origin shapes everything about how the genus handles your tap water. A fish like a livebearer or a goldfish shrugs off a wide range of hardness and pH; an apisto, especially a true blackwater species, does not shrug it off nearly as well.

The good news: species vary a lot in how strict they actually are, and domestic tank-bred lines have been diluting that strictness for decades. Read the individual species guide for the fish you're keeping — this page covers the general framework that applies across the genus, and where the real exceptions are.

pH

Most Apistogramma species want a pH somewhere between 5.0 and 7.0. Where a given species sits in that range depends on how strictly it's tied to true blackwater habitat:

  • Blackwater specialists (nijsseni, steindachneri, and most wild-caught fish generally) want the low end — roughly pH 5.0–6.5 — and are noticeably less forgiving of drift toward neutral. Baenschi and elizabethae push further still, needing true Rio Negro-style water down to pH 4.0.
  • Flexible generalists (macmasteri, hongsloi, and domestic-bred cacatuoides) tolerate a wider band, often comfortably into the high 6s or even neutral 7.0, especially if they were bred and raised in harder water rather than imported as wild fish.

If your tap water tests neutral to slightly alkaline (San Diego municipal water typically runs 7.5–8.2), that doesn't automatically rule out keeping apistos — it means choosing more tolerant, domestic-bred species and stock, or taking steps to soften and acidify the water for the pickier ones.

General Hardness (GH) and Carbonate Hardness (KH)

Hardness matters as much as pH, and the two are connected: water with real carbonate hardness (KH) resists pH change and will slowly pull an acidified tank back toward neutral. Most apistos want soft water — under 10 dGH general hardness, with true specialists preferring under 5 dGH.

If your tap water is hard, options in order of effort:

  • Do nothing, choose tolerant species. Macmasteri and similar domestic-bred fish handle moderately hard water better than their reputation suggests.
  • Cut hard tap water with RO or distilled water to bring GH and KH down to a workable range, then remineralize lightly if needed.
  • Run full RO/DI and remineralize from scratch for full control — the standard approach for anyone serious about blackwater specialists or breeding wild-locality fish.

Temperature

Most Apistogramma species do well between 73–84°F (23–29°C), with individual species guides giving a tighter range. A notable exception is borellii, which comes from further south and tolerates meaningfully cooler water than the rest of the genus — useful if your tank runs on the cool side or you don't want to run a heater hot.

Stability matters more than hitting an exact number. A tank that holds steady at 78°F is better than one that swings between 74°F at night and 82°F during the day because of an undersized or failing heater.

TDS and Why It's Worth Tracking

Total dissolved solids (TDS) is a rough proxy for overall mineral content and gives you a fast daily sanity check without a full water test. Most apistos do best under 150 ppm, with blackwater specialists happier under 100 ppm. A cheap TDS pen is one of the more useful tools you can own if you're running RO water — it tells you at a glance whether your remineralization is consistent from batch to batch.

Blackwater Conditions: What They Actually Do

"Blackwater" isn't just aesthetic — tannin-stained water from driftwood and leaf litter (Indian almond, oak, or beech leaves) genuinely lowers pH, adds mild antimicrobial and antifungal compounds, and seems to reduce stress in species that evolved in exactly that environment. You don't need black-as-tea water to get the benefit; a light tan tint from a modest leaf litter layer does real work.

For blackwater specialists, treat leaf litter and driftwood tannins as a genuine husbandry tool, not decoration. For more tolerant species, it's a nice-to-have that improves color and comfort without being essential.

Drip Acclimating New Fish

Because so much of the trouble with apistos comes from parameter mismatch, how you introduce a new fish matters. Skip the float-the-bag-and-dump approach. Drip acclimate over 45–60 minutes minimum, longer for wild imports or fish coming from noticeably different water than yours — this gives the fish time to adjust gradually rather than facing a chemistry shock on arrival.

Testing: What to Actually Check

You don't need to test everything constantly, but a new tank or a tank with problem fish is worth a real panel:

  • pH — weekly during cycling and setup, then as needed once stable
  • GH/KH — when you first set up your water routine, then periodically to confirm consistency
  • Ammonia, nitrite, nitrate — see our water quality guide for the nitrogen cycle side of things

Don't Overcorrect

The most common mistake with apisto water chemistry isn't neglect — it's overcorrection. Dosing pH-down products aggressively, or changing large volumes of RO water all at once, creates exactly the kind of parameter swing that stresses fish far more than moderately-off-but-stable water. If you need to move parameters, do it gradually, and prioritize consistency over chasing a textbook-perfect number.
