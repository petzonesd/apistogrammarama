Tank Setup

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Short Version
  • 20 gallons is the practical minimum for most species and pairs
  • Sand substrate — apistos sift constantly
  • Broken sightlines (caves, wood, dense planting) reduce aggression
  • Gentle filtration — this genus comes from slow, calm water
  • Dim to moderate lighting, floating plants help

Tank Size

A single pair of most species does fine in a 20-gallon long — the extra floor space of a "long" footprint matters more than depth, since apistos are bottom-and-midwater fish that claim territory across the substrate rather than swimming column. A few notes on sizing by situation:

  • Borellii is the exception on the small end — genuinely nano-friendly, workable in a heavily planted 10–15 gallon for a single pair.
  • Steindachneri is the exception on the large end — a bigger, blunter fish that wants 30+ gallons and real length (40 inches / 100 cm or more).
  • Harems (one male, multiple females) need meaningfully more floor space than a simple pair, so each female can hold her own territory without constant conflict.
  • Species tanks with a breeding project in mind benefit from going a size up from the stated minimum — more room means disputes have somewhere to dissipate instead of escalating.

Substrate

Fine sand is close to a hard requirement across this genus. Apistos sift substrate constantly, searching for food particles with their mouths — coarse gravel can abrade or damage gills and mouths over time, while sand lets the fish do what it evolved to do without injury. Pool filter sand, a fine natural sand, or a sand-capped aquasoil all work; avoid sharp or coarse-grained substrates.

Hardscape

The core idea across every apisto tank: break up sightlines. This genus is territorial toward its own kind, and a bare tank with clear lines of sight turns minor disputes into sustained chases. Driftwood, rockwork, and dense low planting all serve the same purpose — giving fish places to disappear from each other's view.

  • Caves: Every female (in a harem setup) or the pair (in a simple setup) should have access to a cave, coconut hut, or similar enclosed spawning site — ideally more caves than females, so there's always an uncontested option.
  • Driftwood: Adds cover, territory boundaries, and — as it ages — a source of natural tannins that gently soften and acidify the water. See our water parameters guide for more on that effect.
  • Leaf litter: Indian almond, oak, or beech leaves scattered across the substrate recreate the leaf-litter creek beds much of this genus comes from, and double as microfauna habitat that fry will graze on.

Planting

Apistos don't bother plants, which makes them one of the more planted-tank-compatible cichlids you can keep. Dense low-to-midground planting — crypts, java fern, anubias, and similar low-light-tolerant species — gives the fish cover and territory markers without requiring high-tech CO2 injection. Floating plants (frogbit, salvinia, red root floater) are especially useful: they dim the light from above, which most apistos prefer, and give a sense of security that measurably reduces skittishness.

Filtration

This genus comes from slow-moving, often stagnant blackwater habitat — strong current stresses them. Sponge filters are a popular choice for apisto tanks specifically because they provide gentle flow and biological filtration without pulling fry into an intake, which matters if you're breeding. A hang-on-back or canister filter works fine too, just baffle the outflow or turn it down so it isn't creating a current across the whole tank.

Lighting

Moderate to dim lighting suits this genus better than a bright, high-output planted-tank light. If you're running a brighter light for plant growth, floating plants or a diffuser to soften the light column will help the fish relax and color up — apistos consistently show better, more confident color under dimmer conditions than under a stark bright tank.

Cycling Before Fish Go In

Apistos are not a beginner-forgiving species for an uncycled tank — this genus is genuinely sensitive to ammonia and nitrite spikes, more so than hardier community fish. Fully cycle the tank (fishless cycling with an ammonia source, or a slow fish-in cycle with heavy monitoring) before adding apistos, and don't rush stocking a brand-new setup.
