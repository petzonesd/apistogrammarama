Apistogramma atahualpa

Apistogramma atahualpa

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Max 4.2cm (~1.7") SL per FishBase — no detailed keeper account checked for this guide gives an independent adult-size figure
  • Tank: Documented successful breeding in 10- and 20-gallon tanks; no larger-tank account found
  • pH: 5.0–5.5 across two documented breeding accounts; one reading dropped to 3.8 without apparent harm
  • Temperature: 76–82°F across documented accounts
  • Hardness: ~40ppm total hardness in the one account that measured it
  • Temperament: Documented as aggressive between tankmates during one account's shakeout period; not established as unusually aggressive genus-wide
  • Difficulty: Intermediate — straightforward once sourced correctly, but trade misidentification is a real, documented risk (see Sourcing Notes)

Overview

Apistogramma atahualpa carries the name of the last Inca emperor, but for years in the hobby it had no name at all beyond a trade label. Uwe Römer formally described the species in 1997 in The Buntbarsche Bulletin, the journal of the American Cichlid Association (issue no. 182). Before that description existed, per a detailed account from Potomac Valley Aquarium Society (PVAS) breeder Don Kinyon, the fish circulated among hobbyists "known only by 'Species Sunset.'" That trade name — "Sunset" or "A. sp. Sunset" — is still in active use today, sold alongside the valid scientific name rather than replaced by it.

The species belongs to the thick-lipped A. cacatuoides group, per Kinyon's account, placing it in the same broad lineage as this site's own A. cacatuoides and A. macmasteri — a grouping that matters directly here, because trade confusion between atahualpa and macmasteri is itself a documented, ongoing problem (see Sourcing Notes below). Separately, retail photo captions from the German importer and publisher Aqualog have labeled specimens "Apistogramma atahualpa (= sp. aff. payaminonis)," flagging a close taxonomic relationship to A. payaminonis — a name this site's own A. wolli page already covers as part of a documented three-way dealer mixup with payaminonis and feconat. Treat the atahualpa/payaminonis relationship as a separate, Aqualog-documented note rather than the same specific mixup described on the wolli page.

Appearance & Identification

Kinyon's account, the most detailed color description checked for this guide, describes males with a tan-to-brown base body color, a bright yellow cheek and abdomen, blue ventral and anal fins, and an upper dorsal fin striped pale blue above a band of yellow — with the first several dorsal spines elongating as males mature. Both sexes carry black markings at the base of the caudal peduncle, a dotted line along the base of the first few dorsal spines, a slanting eye stripe, and a mid-body lateral stripe that varies from faint to bold depending on the individual and its mood. Females run bright yellow overall with jet-black markings and jet-black ventral fins; during spawning, a female's lateral stripe disappears entirely and is replaced by a solid black spot on her flank.

FishBase gives a single hard number for adult size: 4.2cm (about 1.7 inches) standard length, for males or unsexed fish. Neither PVAS account checked for this guide — Kinyon's or a second account by Francine Bethea — gives an independent adult size figure; both describe fry growth instead (see Breeding below), not final adult measurements. Bethea's account also documents a real sourcing problem tied directly to identification: the wild-caught juveniles she purchased arrived "the typical drab grey," absent from the retail store's own reference books, and neither she nor store staff could sex or confirm them as atahualpa until they colored up weeks later.

Natural Habitat

Locality is a genuine, unresolved conflict between the two sources checked for this guide, not a settled fact this page can simplify. FishBase lists the distribution as "South America: Nanay River basin, Amazon River basin in Brazil." Kinyon's PVAS account, however, places the species in "the Basin of the Rio Ucuyali [Ucayali], in eastern Peru" — a different country and an entirely different river system. Neither source explains or acknowledges the other's claim. The IUCN Red List classifies the species as Data Deficient (assessed April 24, 2014), consistent with how little confirmed field data exists.

No source checked for this guide gives a confirmed wild water-chemistry reading. Kinyon states this directly: "I found no recordings of the water parameters, but I assume it is a black water species" — an assumption, not a documented fact, and one this guide is flagging rather than repeating as settled.

Tank Setup

Documented husbandry for this species comes from two detailed hobbyist breeding accounts rather than a large body of general care writing, so this section reflects what those two keepers actually did.

  • Tank size: Kinyon bred a pair in a 20-gallon tank; Bethea bred a group in a 10-gallon. Neither account documents a larger setup, so treat 10–20 gallons as the documented range rather than a ceiling. See our tank setup guide.
  • Substrate & décor: Bethea's tank used shallow gravel, driftwood with java fern, and halved coconut shells with java moss for cover — a cave-rich setup consistent with genus norms. Kinyon's account used an upturned flower pot as the spawning site.
  • Water chemistry: Both documented accounts ran soft and acidic — pH 5.0–5.5, with Kinyon's tank measuring about 40ppm total hardness and briefly reading pH 3.8 after spawning without apparent harm to the fish. See our water parameters guide for how to reach this range from typical tap water.
  • Filtration & flow: Bethea used a new sponge filter; gentle flow is consistent with genus-standard practice and nothing in either account suggests this species needs anything stronger.

Feeding

Neither documented account gives a detailed adult feeding regimen beyond general live and prepared foods; Kinyon's broader breeding notes mention flake and freeze-dried foods alongside live foods used to condition the breeding pair. Fry in both accounts were raised on microworms and newly-hatched or frozen brine shrimp, with Bethea's fry later graduating to flake and live or frozen brine shrimp as they grew (see Breeding below). For the general genus-standard approach, see our feeding guide.

Temperament & Compatibility

Bethea's account documents real aggression during an early shakeout period: six wild-caught juveniles, initially "extremely shy," turned "very aggressive toward each other" as they matured and colored up, with the author losing several to fighting and jumping before settling on a stable group of two males and three females (the largest of each sex; the rest were sold). One male ended up "crammed underneath the sponge filter with tattered caudal and pectoral fins" and was removed. This matches general Apistogramma norms — rival males sorting out hierarchy in a confined tank — rather than establishing the species as unusually aggressive genus-wide; no account checked for this guide makes that broader claim. As with every species on this site, avoid mixed-species Apistogramma tanks given the documented cross-spawning risk covered on other species pages (see this site's own tucurui page for a specific example).

Breeding

Two independent PVAS accounts document real, successful spawns of Apistogramma atahualpa, though neither records an egg count. In Don Kinyon's account, spawning followed a period of reduced water changes, which he believes triggered it. The female guarded "a clutch of pinkish eggs" laid on the inside wall of an upturned flower pot, defending the cave for six days before leading "about forty free-swimming youngsters" into the open tank. Fry took microworms and newly-hatched brine shrimp from the start. At one month the fry averaged nearly half an inch; shortly after, the tank lost about ten fish to a die-off Kinyon attributes to overfeeding, corrected by lowering the temperature to 75°F and adding young Corydoras. By two months most survivors measured over 1¼ inches. The male was removed from the breeding tank early on, described as too intimidated to feed properly, leaving the female to raise the brood alone.

Francine Bethea's account, from a different tank and apparently a different source population, describes a female partially blocking her cave entrance with gravel — behavior Bethea explicitly notes she can't confirm is typical for the species rather than coincidental. Removing the gravel and watching the female rebuild it convinced her a spawn had occurred; "within two days, the female was leading around 15 fry." Bethea raised algae under grow lights for fry grazing, fed microworms and frozen baby brine shrimp, and later moved fry onto flake and live or frozen brine shrimp as they grew. Between the two accounts, documented fry counts run from roughly 15 to roughly 40 — real numbers from real spawns, but from a small enough sample that neither should be read as a typical brood size for the species. See our general breeding guide for the standard conditioning-to-fry process this genus follows.

Sourcing Notes

Misidentification is this species' single most documented real-world problem, in two separate, independently sourced ways. First: a thread on the apistogramma.com community forum, titled "ID assist: sold as a Sunset, likely a macmasteri," documents a specific case of fish sold under the atahualpa/Sunset trade name that forum members identified as this site's own A. macmasteri instead — a real, if informal, case of trade mislabeling between two genuinely different species. Second, and separately: Bethea's PVAS account describes buying "a wild shipment of Sunsets" from a store where staff "didn't know any more about them than I did" — unidentified, unsexed juvenile fish sold under a trade name with no confirmation of species at the point of sale. Between these two documented problems, buy from a seller who can either show you adult coloration directly or vouch for collection/breeder lineage, rather than trusting the "Sunset" trade name alone. See our general sourcing guide for more on vetting a seller.

Final Thoughts

Atahualpa is a genuinely interesting case of a long-circulated trade fish that still hasn't fully shaken its placeholder identity — sold today under the same "Sunset" name it carried before it had a scientific name at all, and still, per a real forum case, sometimes sold as the wrong species entirely. Two independent PVAS breeding accounts show it's a tractable soft-water breeder once correctly identified, even if neither account nor FishBase can settle where in South America it actually comes from. If the macmasteri-confusion risk gives you pause, this site's own A. macmasteri page covers the species most often mistaken for it, and A. wolli covers a separate, similarly tangled trade-ID story in the same genus.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Apistogramma atahualpa named for?

Atahualpa, the last ruler of the Inca empire, who died in 1533. Uwe Römer formally described the species in 1997 in The Buntbarsche Bulletin (ACA) no. 182. Before that, per a detailed Potomac Valley Aquarium Society (PVAS) account by breeder Don Kinyon, it circulated in the hobby "known only by 'Species Sunset'" — the trade name 'Sunset' or 'A. sp. Sunset' is still used today.

Is Apistogramma atahualpa often sold misidentified?

Yes, in two separate documented ways. A thread on the apistogramma.com forum, titled "ID assist: sold as a Sunset, likely a macmasteri," shows fish sold under the atahualpa/Sunset trade name turning out to be this site's own A. macmasteri instead. Separately, a PVAS account by Francine Bethea describes buying "wild shipment of Sunsets" from a store whose own staff couldn't identify or sex them — the fish arrived as drab, unidentified juveniles with no name beyond the trade label until they colored up.

Where does Apistogramma atahualpa come from?

Sources disagree. FishBase lists the Nanay River basin in the Amazon basin in Brazil. But Don Kinyon's PVAS account places the species in "the Basin of the Rio Ucayali, in eastern Peru" — a different country and a different river system, with no explanation offered for the conflict. This guide notes both rather than picking one.

What water parameters does Apistogramma atahualpa need?

Two independent PVAS breeding accounts agree on soft, acidic water: Don Kinyon bred a pair at pH 5.5 falling to 5.0, roughly 40ppm total hardness, 76–82°F; Francine Bethea's tank ran pH 5.0, 78°F. Neither account nor FishBase documents a confirmed wild-water reading — Kinyon states plainly he "found no recordings of the water parameters" and was only assuming a blackwater origin.
