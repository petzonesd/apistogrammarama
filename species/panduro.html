Apistogramma panduro

Panduro Dwarf Cichlid

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Male to 3" (7.5 cm), female to 2.2" (5.5 cm)
  • Tank: 20 gallons minimum, 24"+ long for a pair
  • pH: 4.0–6.5 — demanding blackwater chemistry
  • Temperature: 72–84°F (22–29°C)
  • Hardness: Very soft, 0–5 dGH
  • Temperament: Can be aggressive toward conspecifics; pairs are often monogamous
  • Difficulty: Advanced

Overview

Apistogramma panduro is a blackwater dwarf cichlid from the Río Ucayali drainage and Río Tahuayo in Peru's Loreto Region, prized for a soft blue body color and lumped into the same water-chemistry bracket as baenschi and elizabethae. It inhabits slow tributaries with heavy leaf litter, the same kind of tannin-stained, mineral-poor water those two species come from, which is why it demands similarly extreme parameters in the tank despite looking nothing like either one.

The trade name "blue panda cichlid" causes real confusion, since Apistogramma nijsseni already carries the "panda dwarf cichlid" nickname for its black eye patches. Panduro's nickname has nothing to do with panda markings — it's simply a play on the species name itself. A distinctive, slightly underslung lower jaw that projects past the upper jaw is the more reliable field mark for telling it apart from similarly colored apistos at a glance.

Tank Setup

Give a pair at least 20 gallons with 24 inches of length — SeriouslyFish lists a workable minimum footprint of 24 × 12 inches (about 14 gallons) for one pair, but more swimming and sightline-breaking room reduces stress and aggression considerably.

  • Substrate: Fine, dark sand. Panduro forages leaf litter and substrate for small invertebrates.
  • Leaf litter: Strongly recommended. Indian almond or oak leaves replicate the tannin-stained water this species evolved in and help hold pH down naturally.
  • Hardscape: Driftwood, root tangles, and dense planting with multiple caves — this species wants to hide as much as it wants to swim.
  • Filtration: Gentle flow. These are slow-tributary fish, not current-lovers.
  • Lighting: Dim, with floating plants to cut glare — bright, bare tanks wash out the blue coloration and stress the fish.
  • Water chemistry: RO/DI water remineralized to very soft, acidic parameters is close to mandatory here; most municipal tap water is too hard and alkaline to get panduro breeding reliably.

Feeding

Primarily carnivorous in the wild, hunting benthic invertebrates in leaf litter. In the aquarium, offer live or frozen Artemia, daphnia, and bloodworms as the backbone of the diet; most individuals eventually learn to take a quality micro-pellet or flake as well, though live and frozen foods keep color and breeding condition noticeably better than dry food alone.

Temperament & Compatibility

More assertive toward its own species than toward tankmates. SeriouslyFish and retailer sources agree established pairs tend to settle into stable monogamy, but adding new panduro to a mature pair's territory — or housing two pairs in anything short of a very large, heavily broken-up tank — invites real aggression. As with bitaeniata and the genus's other feistier members, captive-bred and captive-raised stock generally adapts to community life more readily than wild imports.

Peaceful, bottom-avoiding community fish — small tetras, rasboras, and slim catfish — make reasonable tankmates in a large enough tank. Dwarf shrimp are not safe alongside adult panduro.

Breeding

Conditioning

Condition the pair on live and frozen foods for two to three weeks before expecting spawns. As with baenschi and elizabethae, breeding success here tracks water chemistry far more tightly than it does for easier species — hitting the low pH and very soft hardness on paper matters less than holding it steady.

Spawning

A cave spawner. The female selects a cave, cranny, or similarly enclosed space, deposits her eggs inside, and takes over nearly all brood defense once laid. In a smaller tank, some keepers remove the male after spawning to prevent him from harassing the guarding female — a precaution worth having on hand given how aggressive this species can run.

Fry Care

Fry are tiny even by apisto standards and need infusoria or a liquid fry food for the first several days before stepping up to newly hatched brine shrimp and microworms. Keep water changes small and frequent rather than large and infrequent — soft, acidic water drifts quickly, and fry tolerate that drift far worse than the adults do.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Panduro is a specialist import rather than a mass-farmed staple, so expect to source it through dedicated apisto breeders or specialty retailers rather than a typical big-box fish wall. Ask whether stock is captive-bred or wild-caught — captive-bred fish are already adjusted to aquarium food and tend to settle in with less stress — and look for active, evenly colored individuals with no clamped fins or washed-out color, a common sign of water chemistry that's too far from this species' narrow comfort zone.

Final Thoughts

If you've already got baenschi or elizabethae breeding reliably and want another extreme-blackwater species for the collection, panduro is a natural next pick — just don't confuse its "blue panda" nickname with nijsseni's actual panda markings when you're shopping for it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Apistogramma panduro the same as the "panda" dwarf cichlid?

No, and it's a common mix-up. Apistogramma nijsseni is the species actually nicknamed the panda dwarf cichlid, for the black patches around its eyes. Panduro picked up the trade nickname "blue panda cichlid" independently, from the sound of its own species name, not from any panda-like marking.

How big does Apistogramma panduro get?

Males reach about 3 inches (7.5 cm) and females about 2.2 inches (5.5 cm), putting it in the same size bracket as bitaeniata and agassizii rather than with the smaller nano species like borellii.

What water parameters does panduro need?

Soft and acidic — pH 4.0 to 6.5, hardness under 5 dGH — on par with the genus's most demanding blackwater specialists like baenschi and elizabethae. This is not a species to attempt straight out of hard tap water.

Is panduro aggressive?

It can be, particularly toward other panduro. Established pairs tend to stay monogamous and settle down, but introducing new conspecifics to a mature pair's territory often goes poorly. Captive-raised stock generally adjusts to community life better than wild-caught imports.

Is panduro a good beginner apisto?

No. Between the extreme water chemistry and the conspecific aggression, it belongs with baenschi and elizabethae as an advanced-keeper species, not a first or second apisto. Start with cacatuoides and work up to this one.
