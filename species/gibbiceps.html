Apistogramma gibbiceps

Apistogramma gibbiceps

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Male to roughly 3" (7–7.5 cm), female noticeably smaller — among the largest in the genus
  • Tank: 24"×12" (~20 gal) minimum footprint for a pair; the larger body size rewards extra room
  • pH: 4.5–6.5 — soft and acidic, but more flexible than the genus's true blackwater extremists
  • Temperature: 77–84°F (25–29°C)
  • Hardness: Soft, 1–5 dGH
  • Temperament: Males can be aggressive toward rival males; otherwise unremarkable
  • Difficulty: Advanced — not for its water chemistry, but because it's genuinely hard to find

Overview

Apistogramma gibbiceps carries a scientific name that means roughly "hump head" or "rams head" — and the name is a mistake. Meinken described the species in 1969 from a specimen with a visibly deformed, swollen skull, and built the name around that feature. Later examination suggests the deformity was almost certainly an individual abnormality — from age, poor husbandry before it reached Meinken, or an artifact of preservation — rather than anything typical of the species. Every gibbiceps imported since has an ordinary head profile. Once a species name is published, though, it isn't changed just because the type specimen turns out to be unrepresentative, so the misleading name has stuck for over fifty years.

The fish itself comes from the upper and middle Rio Negro and Rio Branco basins in Brazil, generally in soft, acidic, clear-to-blackwater conditions over humus-rich substrate — broadly similar habitat to the cardinal tetra, which shares its range. Unlike the genus's most extreme softwater specialists such as baenschi or eremnopyge, gibbiceps isn't confined to true blackwater and adapts reasonably well across a wider band of soft, acidic conditions.

What actually sets gibbiceps apart in practice isn't its biology — it's that almost nobody keeps it. This is a wholesale-trade fish far more than a retail-shelf one; see Sourcing Notes below.

Color & Identification

Males are polychromatic — current breeding and import stock includes both blue-headed and yellow-headed color forms, over a silvery-gray body carrying iridescent blue, yellow, and red highlights plus dark facial markings. Selective breeding from yellow-headed lines has reportedly produced red-headed males within a few generations, which is a meaningfully faster color shift than most apisto color-form work.

Belly markings are mood-dependent rather than fixed: the same individual fish can show vertical bars, a single horizontal stripe, or a round spot centered on the body depending on its state. A dark bar beneath the eye appears specifically when the fish is aggressive, making it a useful at-a-glance read on a male's mood before you decide whether it's safe to add more fish to the tank.

Tank Setup

Because gibbiceps runs larger-bodied than most commonly kept apistos, give a pair at minimum a 24 × 12 inch footprint (roughly 20 US gallons) rather than the 20-gallon-long minimum that suffices for smaller species like cacatuoides.

  • Substrate: Soft sand.
  • Hardscape: Build a complex layout with numerous caves and alcoves — flowerpots, driftwood tunnels, or rock caves all work. Males can be territorial toward their own species and benefit from having multiple potential territories to claim rather than one obvious best cave.
  • Planting and light: Moderate, filtered light suits the soft-water plants that tolerate this chemistry — Microsorum, Java moss, and Cryptocoryne are all good fits, with floating plants to cut glare further.
  • Water chemistry: Soft and acidic, but more forgiving than the genus's true extremists — you don't need to chase pH 4.0 here. RO/DI blended with tap, or straight tap in a naturally soft-water area, can both work depending on your local water.
  • Filtration: Gentle flow. This isn't a current-loving species.

Feeding

Omnivorous with a carnivorous lean. Offer a varied diet of live or frozen foods — daphnia, bloodworm, and Artemia all work well — supplemented with quality dry foods once the fish settles in. Feed small portions your fish can finish within two to three minutes rather than one large daily feeding; several small feedings keep water quality more stable and condition fish better for breeding.

Temperament & Compatibility

Generally an unremarkable bottom-dweller day to day — active, not shy, and tolerant of non-territorial tankmates. The friction point is between rival males: as a larger-bodied species, an unchallenged male can be genuinely aggressive toward another male sharing the tank, more so than the size difference alone might suggest. One male with one or several females works better than attempting to house two males together in anything short of a large, heavily broken-up tank.

Breeding

Gibbiceps follows the standard Apistogramma breeding pattern described in our genus-wide breeding guide — cave spawning, maternal fry care, and a male that holds territory nearby rather than guarding the brood directly. Specific numbers for egg counts and fry timelines for this particular species are thin in the literature, a direct consequence of how few hobbyists have bred it compared to a species like cacatuoides or nijsseni with decades of documented spawns. What is documented is the color-form breeding: yellow-headed stock has been selectively bred toward red-headed males within a handful of generations, suggesting the head-color trait responds readily to selection — a project worth pursuing if you can actually source a confirmed pair.

Sourcing Notes

This is the real difficulty with gibbiceps, not its water chemistry. German wholesaler Aquarium Glaser — one of the few importers handling the species at all — supplies it exclusively to the wholesale trade, not individual hobbyists, and receives it only occasionally ("once more," as one of their own stock updates put it). Brazilian exporters offer it from time to time, but few shipments reach the US, and when they do, specimens frequently arrive too young to sex reliably, with import batches often skewing male. Few hobbyist breeders offer surplus stock as a result.

Realistically, your best path to a confirmed, sexable pair is a specialist importer or dedicated apisto breeder rather than a local fish store — see our general sourcing guide for the questions worth asking either source, and our community page for where to find dedicated apisto breeders and import-tracking hobbyists.

Final Thoughts

Gibbiceps is an odd case: a fish named for a defect it's never actually had, from a habitat that's genuinely more forgiving than half this genus, that almost nobody can find. If you've got the space for a larger apisto and the patience to track one down through specialist channels rather than a store shelf, it rewards you with a bigger, polychromatic fish and a color-form breeding project — blue-headed, yellow-headed, and selectively-bred red-headed males — that most keepers never get the chance to try. If sourcing one doesn't pan out, steindachneri is the closest thing on this site to a similarly large-bodied, more available alternative.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is it called Apistogramma gibbiceps if it doesn't have a "hump head"?

Because the name describes a trait the species doesn't actually have. Meinken described gibbiceps in 1969 from a specimen with a deformed, swollen skull — gibbiceps means roughly "hump head" or "rams head" in Latin. That deformity was almost certainly an individual abnormality from age, poor care, or the preservation process, not a feature of the species. Every gibbiceps imported since has a normal head profile, but the misleading name stuck because species names, once published, aren't changed just because the original description turns out to be based on an atypical fish.

What water parameters does Apistogramma gibbiceps need?

Soft and acidic: pH 4.5–6.5, 1–5 dGH, and 77–84°F (25–29°C). It comes from the generally soft, clear-to-blackwater middle and upper Rio Negro and Rio Branco basins, though unlike the genus's most extreme specialists it isn't restricted to true blackwater and tolerates a bit more flexibility within that soft-acidic range.

How big does Apistogramma gibbiceps get?

Among the larger species in the genus. Males are reported approaching 3 inches (around 7–7.5 cm), noticeably bigger than the 2–2.5 inch range typical of most commonly kept apistos, while females stay distinctly smaller. Reported figures vary somewhat between sources, which is itself a symptom of how little this species circulates in the hobby compared to cacatuoides or agassizii.

Can you actually buy Apistogramma gibbiceps?

Rarely, and almost never through general retail. German wholesaler Aquarium Glaser, one of the few importers that handles it at all, supplies it strictly to the wholesale trade rather than individual hobbyists, and receives shipments only occasionally. Brazilian exporters offer it from time to time, but few import batches make it to the US, and specimens often arrive too small to sex reliably. A specialist apisto importer or breeder, found through a dedicated cichlid society or the online communities on our community page, is a far more realistic route than a local fish store.

What do male Apistogramma gibbiceps look like?

Polychromatic. Current breeding stock includes both blue-headed and yellow-headed males, on a silvery-gray body with iridescent blue, yellow, and red highlights and dark facial markings. Belly markings also shift with mood — vertical bars, a single horizontal stripe, or a round central spot can each appear on the same fish — and a dark bar below the eye shows up specifically when the fish is aggressive. Selective breeding from yellow-headed lines has reportedly produced red-headed males within a few generations.
