Apistogramma eunotus

Apistogramma eunotus

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Male to 2.2" (5.5 cm) SL, female to 1.6" (4 cm) SL
  • Tank: 18"×12" (~11 gal) minimum footprint for a pair; more for groups
  • pH: 5.5–7.0 for general keeping — avoid pushing below 5.5
  • Temperature: 73–84°F (23–29°C)
  • Hardness: Soft, 0–5 dGH for keeping; tolerates harder water than most apistos
  • Temperament: Male can be pushy toward the female; keep away from other Apistogramma
  • Difficulty: Intermediate–Advanced (chemistry is forgiving; sourcing and compatibility are the real challenge)

Overview

Apistogramma eunotus is a large-bodied, color-variable dwarf cichlid from the Río Ucayali drainage near Pucallpa, Peru, and surrounding tributaries reaching east into the Japurá basin — and it's one of the few apistos that doesn't actually want true blackwater. Wild populations have been recorded in blackwater, clearwater, and whitewater habitats alike, and professional breeders report it avoids the highly acidic water (below pH 5.5) that most of the genus is built around, breeding more reliably in slightly alkaline, medium-hardness conditions instead. That makes it a genuinely useful counterexample on a site that otherwise leans hard on the "soft, acidic water" framework for this genus — see our water parameters guide for where it fits against the rest of the family.

Males are polychromatic: several different color forms can occur within a single wild population, and the most sought-after is the "orange-tailed" morph prized by specialist keepers. That nickname describes a color form, not a fixed trait of the species — some import batches contain no orange-tailed males at all, so it isn't something to count on without seeing the actual fish or batch photos first.

Tank Setup

A single pair is comfortable in a footprint of about 18 × 12 inches (roughly 11 US gallons) at minimum; a group, or a pair you want fully settled and spawning, does better with more floor space and visual breaks.

  • Substrate: Fine sand. This species digs, and breeding behavior in particular relies on being able to excavate around its chosen cave.
  • Planting: Heavy and low-to-moderate light — Microsorum, Taxiphyllum (Java moss), Cryptocoryne, and Anubias all suit the dim conditions this species prefers. Floating plants help diffuse light further.
  • Hardscape: Bogwood for tannins plus several small caves — include at least one cave too small for the male to fit, so the female has a retreat he can't follow her into.
  • Leaf litter: Worth adding. It releases tannins and supports the microbial growth that can supplement fry diets, even though this species doesn't need the acidic extremes leaf litter is often used to chase.
  • Filtration: Weak to moderate flow — a filter in the 80–125 lph range suits a modest setup. These are slow-tributary fish, not current-lovers.
  • Water chemistry: Unusually forgiving for the genus. You don't need an RO/DI setup chasing pH 5 here the way you would for baenschi or elizabethae — moderate, stable water in the 5.5–7.0 range works, and breeders report better results avoiding the acidic extreme rather than pursuing it.

Feeding

Carnivorous, hunting benthic invertebrates in the wild. In the aquarium, offer live or frozen Artemia, daphnia, and bloodworm as the staple diet. Most individuals will also take dried food once settled, and pellets are generally accepted more readily than flakes.

Temperament & Compatibility

Captive-raised stock generally adapts to community life reasonably well. Wild-caught imports are a different story — they do best kept alone or alongside small, unobtrusive dither fish such as Nannostomus pencilfish, and shouldn't be mixed with other Apistogramma species regardless of origin. Within a pair, the male can be the bigger behavioral risk: he's more likely to push the female than to bother tankmates, so watch the pair dynamic as closely as you'd watch for outside aggression.

As with the genus generally, dwarf shrimp aren't a safe combination with an adult eunotus.

Breeding

Conditioning

Condition on live and frozen foods for one to two weeks before expecting spawns. Because this species breeds best away from the acidic extreme, resist the instinct to push water chemistry toward the low pH that works for baenschi or panduro — moderate, stable parameters on the neutral-to-slightly-alkaline side of this species' range tend to produce better results.

Spawning

A substrate spawner that lays eggs in crevices and cavities among the décor rather than in the open. The female takes over nearly all brood defense once eggs are laid and can become genuinely aggressive in the process — in a smaller tank, plan to remove the male after spawning if he starts getting harassed rather than waiting to see how it plays out.

Fry Care

Fine sandy substrate matters here specifically because the fish digs around its chosen spawning cave — keep that in mind when planning substrate depth near caves. Fry take infusoria or a liquid fry food initially before stepping up to newly hatched brine shrimp and microworms, the same progression as other apistos on this site.

Sourcing Notes

This isn't a species you'll find on a typical retail wall — it moves through specialist apisto breeders and importers, and the dedicated hobbyist community at forums like apistogramma.com is the most active place to track down stock or ask about current availability. Because the orange-tailed morph isn't guaranteed in every batch, ask for photos of the actual male(s) on offer rather than buying on the species name alone, and see our sourcing guide for the general questions worth asking any seller.

Final Thoughts

Eunotus is worth a look specifically because it breaks the genus's own rules: a large, handsome apisto that doesn't demand the extreme blackwater chemistry its relatives do, provided you resist the urge to chase low pH anyway. The real challenges are patience in sourcing a good male and getting the pair dynamic right — not the water chemistry most keepers brace for going in. If you've already got the genus-standard blackwater specialists like baenschi or elizabethae running, eunotus is a useful change of pace rather than another tank chasing the same extreme numbers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes Apistogramma eunotus different from most other Apistogramma species?

It's one of the few apistos that doesn't want true blackwater. Wild eunotus turn up in blackwater, clearwater, and even whitewater habitats, and professional breeders report it actually avoids highly acidic conditions (below pH 5.5) and breeds more reliably in slightly alkaline, medium-hardness water. That makes it an outlier in a genus where extreme soft-acid chemistry is the norm.

What is the "orange-tailed" Apistogramma eunotus?

Eunotus males are polychromatic, meaning several different male color forms can occur even within one wild population. The orange-tailed morph is the most prized in the hobby, but it's genuinely uncommon — some import batches arrive with no orange-tailed males at all, so it isn't something a buyer can count on sight-unseen.

How big does Apistogramma eunotus get?

Males reach roughly 2.2 inches (5.5 cm) standard length and females about 1.6 inches (4 cm), making it one of the larger-bodied species on this site — in the same bracket as bitaeniata and agassizii rather than the smaller nano species.

What water parameters does eunotus actually need?

For general aquarium keeping, aim for pH 5.5–7.0, 0–5 dGH, and 73–84°F (23–29°C) — comfortably soft and mild rather than extreme. If you're specifically trying to breed it, lean toward the neutral-to-slightly-alkaline, medium-hardness end of that range rather than pushing the water acidic, since breeders report better results there than with the ultra-soft, low-pH water other apistos demand.

Is Apistogramma eunotus a good fit for a community tank?

Captive-raised stock adapts reasonably well to community life. Wild-caught imports do best alone or with small, unobtrusive dither fish like Nannostomus pencilfish. Either way, avoid housing it with other Apistogramma species, and expect the male to be pushier toward the female than toward tankmates.
