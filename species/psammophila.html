Apistogramma psammophila

Apistogramma psammophila

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: To 1.1" (2.8cm SL), per the original description — one of the smaller Apistogramma species; sexually mature from roughly 0.8–1.2" (2–3cm)
  • Tank: 20 gallons minimum, fine sand substrate
  • pH: Not documented from wild habitat beyond "very acidic"; commercial guidance is 5.0–7.0 (6.0–6.5 for breeding)
  • Temperature: Not documented from wild habitat; commercial guidance is 73–80°F (23–27°C)
  • Hardness: Wild habitat described as "extremely soft"; commercial guidance is roughly 2–10 dGH (~5 dH for breeding)
  • Temperament: Peaceful toward other species; reportedly severely intolerant of its own kind
  • Difficulty: Advanced/specialist — described only in 2019, wholesale-only, and endangered in the wild

Overview

Apistogramma psammophila is one of this genus's newest described species — and the fish that quietly split off part of what hobbyists had been calling this site's own A. diplotaenia. Wolfgang Staeck and Ingo Schindler formally described it in 2019 in Vertebrate Zoology (69(1):103–110), published by the Senckenberg Gesellschaft für Naturforschung, from specimens collected in the lower Rio Atabapo drainage in Colombia's Departamento Guainía and Venezuela's Estado Amazonas. The name is straightforward Greek: psammos ("sand") plus philos ("loving"), after the exposed sandy river beach where the type series was collected.

The description's own framing matters here: psammophila is identified as the closest known relative of A. diplotaenia, the Rio Negro blackwater specialist already covered on this site. Before 2019, that resemblance went deeper than taxonomy — German wholesale importer Aquarium Glaser notes that juveniles of the two species cannot be told apart at all without knowing exactly where they were collected, and that diplotaenia's coloration had been considered "unmistakable" right up until psammophila's description showed there was a near-identical sibling hiding in a different river system. It's a mirror image of the identification problems this site documents elsewhere in the genus — see uaupesi and iniridae, or the juvenile mislabeling risk flagged on this site's own kullanderi page — except here the confusion runs between two now-separately-described species rather than a described species and an unnamed one.

Natural Habitat

Psammophila is known from the lower Rio Atabapo drainage, with Aquatic Republic placing the type locality near the mouth of Caño Chamuchina in Guainía Department, Colombia (roughly 3°47'22"N, 67°38'12"W); the original description also records the species from the Venezuelan side of the same drainage, in Estado Amazonas. FishBase characterizes the habitat as blackwater — clear, very acidic, and extremely soft, "tea-colored" water — with specimens collected several meters out from an exposed sandy beach, the detail the species name itself refers to. No exact pH, hardness, or temperature figures from the wild habitat appear in the original description or in any aquarium-specific source checked for this guide.

Color & Identification

The original description diagnoses psammophila by two dark horizontal stripes along the body, two pectoral spots, a suborbital stripe that widens noticeably toward the belly, and the absence of a caudal spot. Adult males carry long filamentous extensions on the soft dorsal and anal fins that nearly reach the back edge of the tail, plus a pelvic-fin filament reaching roughly to the middle of the anal-fin base. The two features most useful for telling it apart from its close relative diplotaenia are a lanceolate (pointed) rather than rounded caudal fin — reliable only once a male is fully grown — and an irregular pattern of 6–8 short, vertical dark bars low on the body, which diplotaenia never develops.

That second mark comes with a real caveat, per Aquarium Glaser's own account of handling the species: the belly bars are mood-dependent. They're most visible on a calm, settled fish, and they fade or disappear almost entirely under stress or during aggressive encounters — which means a nervous or recently-shipped fish can look deceptively like plain diplotaenia even to an experienced eye. Juveniles of the two species offer no reliable visual cues at all; without confirmed collection data, there's genuinely no way to be sure which one you're looking at.

Tank Setup

  • Tank size: Commercial care sources recommend a 20-gallon minimum. No wild-habitat-specific tank-size guidance exists in the sources checked for this guide.
  • Substrate: Fine sand is specifically recommended, consistent with a species named for its sandy-beach habitat — this is one apisto profile on this site where the substrate choice has a direct taxonomic justification, not just genus-standard convention.
  • Hardscape & cover: Driftwood, rocks, and dense, hardy planting, with as many visual barriers as the footprint allows — a detail that matters more here than usual given the species' documented intraspecific intolerance (see Temperament below).
  • Filtration & flow: Gentle flow is recommended; avoid filtration that creates strong current. See our water quality guide for genus-standard stability advice.
  • Water changes: No species-specific schedule was found in the sources checked for this guide. Given the closely related diplotaenia's documented need for frequent, modest water changes to hold soft, acidic water stable, treating that as a reasonable starting point for psammophila is a sensible default rather than a confirmed figure.

Feeding

Commercial care sources describe psammophila as primarily carnivorous on small invertebrates despite an "omnivore" label in some quick-reference tables — live and frozen foods such as Artemia, bloodworms, tubifex, and daphnia are recommended, supplemented with quality sinking pellets and flakes. It forages mainly near the substrate, consistent with genus-standard feeding advice used throughout this site. See our general feeding guide for broader conditioning strategy ahead of a breeding attempt.

Temperament & Compatibility

Toward other species, psammophila is described as peaceful, with the usual apisto caution around breeding-pair territoriality. The real concern is within its own species: Aquarium Glaser specifically warns that individuals are highly incompatible with each other, and unusually so — not through visible damage from fighting, but through a kind of chronic social stress that appears to weaken the immune system enough on its own to trigger serious disease. That's a different failure mode than the aggression warnings given elsewhere on this site, and it argues for keeping this species as a single pair or a carefully managed one-male, several-female harem with generous visual barriers rather than assuming a larger group will simply sort itself out. As with every other species on this site, avoid mixing with other Apistogramma species; suitable calm dither tankmates include Corydoras, small tetras, hatchetfish, pencilfish, and suckermouth catfish.

Breeding

Psammophila is a cave spawner, consistent with the rest of the genus and with its close relative diplotaenia. No species-specific breeding account was found in the primary literature checked for this guide, so the genus-standard approach documented elsewhere on this site applies here: offer a dedicated spawning surface such as an upturned flowerpot or similar cave, and nudge water toward pH 6.0–6.5 at roughly 5 dH to help trigger a spawn. Genus-wide patterns for this kind of cave spawner put a typical clutch at 40–60 eggs laid on the cave ceiling, hatching in 2–4 days depending on temperature, with the female guarding the eggs and fry and eventually leading them out of the cave. Given this species' documented intraspecific intolerance, removing the male after spawning is a reasonable precaution in anything smaller than a large, well-structured tank. Follow our general breeding guide for the fuller conditioning-to-fry process.

Sourcing Notes

Psammophila is a genuinely recent arrival in the hobby — described only in 2019 and still moving almost entirely through specialist wholesale channels rather than general retail. German importer Aquarium Glaser, which stocks the species under its own code, calls it "precious" and recommends it only for experienced keepers, not as a casual purchase. The identification risk documented above is the real sourcing hazard here: because juvenile psammophila and diplotaenia cannot be told apart without confirmed locality data, ask any seller specifically how an individual's identity was established — a seller who can't answer that may be selling an unconfirmed guess rather than a verified species. See our general sourcing guide for more on vetting a seller's claims, and compare this to the similar juvenile-identification caution given on this site's kullanderi profile.

Final Thoughts

Psammophila is a good example of how young this genus's taxonomy still is: a scientifically distinct species hiding for decades behind the look of a better-known relative, only separated out in 2019 — and even now, reliably distinguishable only in calm, fully grown males. It pairs a narrow, IUCN-Endangered wild range with a documented intolerance of its own kind in captivity, which makes it a specialist's fish rather than a second or third apisto for most keepers. If you already keep this site's own diplotaenia and want its closest known relative as a genuine taxonomic companion piece rather than a repeat of the same fish, psammophila is worth tracking down from a dedicated apisto importer — just confirm the identification before you buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Apistogramma psammophila, and how is it related to diplotaenia?

Apistogramma psammophila is a dwarf cichlid formally described in 2019 by Wolfgang Staeck and Ingo Schindler in the journal Vertebrate Zoology (69(1):103–110), from the lower Rio Atabapo drainage in Colombia and Venezuela. It's the closest known relative of this site's own A. diplotaenia, a Rio Negro species from Brazil — before 2019, fish matching either species' general look were often assumed to simply be diplotaenia, since the two are strikingly similar and juveniles of the pair genuinely cannot be told apart without knowing exactly where they were collected. The species name comes from Greek psammos ("sand") and philos ("loving"), after the exposed sandy river beach near where the type specimens were collected.

How do you tell Apistogramma psammophila apart from diplotaenia?

In grown males, the most reliable mark is an irregular row of 6–8 short, vertical dark bars on the lower belly, which diplotaenia never shows — but German wholesale importer Aquarium Glaser notes these bars are mood-dependent: a stressed or aggressive fish can hide them almost completely, and they show up best on a calm, relaxed individual. The original scientific description also lists a lanceolate (pointed) caudal fin in psammophila versus a rounded one in diplotaenia, but that's only visible once males are fully grown. Juveniles of the two species cannot be reliably separated by appearance alone — origin is the only sure way.

What water parameters does Apistogramma psammophila need?

The original description characterizes its native habitat only qualitatively — clear, very acidic, extremely soft, tea-colored blackwater over a sandy bottom — without publishing exact pH, hardness, or temperature figures. Commercial care sources selling the species give a working range of pH 5.0–7.0, 73–80°F (23–27°C), and soft water (roughly 2–10 dGH), narrowing toward pH 6.0–6.5 at about 5 dH for breeding, which matches the genus-standard breeding guidance given for this site's other apistos. Treat the commercial figures as a working default rather than confirmed wild data. See our water parameters guide for building soft, acidic water safely.

Is Apistogramma psammophila available in the aquarium trade?

Only recently and only through specialist channels. Described in 2019, it's described by one aquarium retailer as a "recent addition to the aquarium trade," and German wholesale importer Aquarium Glaser — which does stock it under its own code — calls it a fish for "experienced specialists" rather than a general retail item. Because juveniles can't be reliably told apart from diplotaenia without confirmed collection data, ask any seller specifically how the identification was confirmed before buying. See our general sourcing guide for more on vetting a seller's claims.

Is Apistogramma psammophila hard to keep, and is it endangered?

Yes, on both counts. FishBase lists it as IUCN Endangered (assessed 2020), reflecting its narrow known range in the lower Rio Atabapo drainage. On the keeping side, Aquarium Glaser specifically warns that individuals are highly intolerant of each other — not through visible fighting damage, but through chronic stress that appears to weaken the immune system enough to trigger serious disease. That makes stocking density and the number of visual barriers in the tank arguably more important for this species than exact water chemistry.
