Apistogramma wapisana

Apistogramma wapisana

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: 1.1" (2.8cm SL) male/unsexed, 1.14" (2.9cm SL) female — females slightly larger
  • Tank: Not documented precisely; genus-standard 15–20g used as a working default
  • pH: 5.5–6.0 native; 6.0–6.5 for breeding attempts
  • Temperature: Not documented precisely; native habitat described as "medium to comparatively high"
  • Hardness: Soft, roughly 5°dH for breeding attempts
  • Temperament: Not documented species-specifically
  • Difficulty: Advanced — extremely rare in the hobby, no confirmed breeding reports found

Overview

Apistogramma wapisana is one of the genuinely rare entries in this site's library — described in 2006 from a single small forest stream near Boa Vista, Roraima, in far northern Brazil, and still, by the account of the species reference site Cichlidae.com, "a very rare species" today. Uwe Römer, Ingo Hahn, and Anthony Conrad formally described it in Cichlid Atlas 2, naming it for the Wapishana — also spelled Mawayana — an indigenous people of the Roraima region where the type specimens were collected. Cichlidae.com classifies it within its own "Wapisana group" inside the genus.

This is the second species in this site's library co-described by Ingo Hahn, alongside this site's own A. barlowi (2008) — both projects involving Uwe Römer, whose taxonomic work also produced this site's own A. baenschi. Unlike barlowi, which settled into a recognizable trade identity, wapisana has stayed a near-ghost in the hobby: the clearest documentation found for this guide is a single photograph of a male in the aquarium of Mike Wise, a longtime Apistogramma authority and forum moderator.

Natural Habitat

The type locality, per the original description, is a small forest stream (igarapé) less than a kilometer behind the bridge on the road running east out of Boa Vista, the capital of Roraima state — a city that sits directly on the Rio Branco, the major right-bank tributary that carries this region's drainage into the Rio Negro and, eventually, the Amazon. FishBase describes the habitat as smaller, muddy streams influenced by whitewater, with a thick layer of leaf litter and medium-to-comparatively-high water temperatures — notably not the clear, tannin-stained blackwater that several other species on this site, like elizabethae or baenschi, specialize in.

Tank Setup

  • Tank size: Not documented precisely in the sources checked for this guide. A genus-standard 15–20 gallon tank, as used for similarly small species elsewhere on this site, is a reasonable working default. See our tank setup guide.
  • Substrate & leaf litter: The native habitat's heavy leaf-litter layer over a muddy substrate is worth replicating — fine, soft substrate with a generous bed of leaf litter rather than bare rock or gravel.
  • Water chemistry: FishBase lists native-habitat pH at 5.5 to 6.0, milder than this genus's extreme blackwater specialists. A breeding-specific data sheet recommends pH 6.0–6.5 and soft water around 5°dH for spawning attempts. See our water parameters guide.
  • Filtration & flow: Not documented for this species specifically; the native stream habitat is described as whitewater-influenced rather than still, so moderate rather than stagnant flow is a reasonable default. See our water quality guide.
  • Breeding surface: One breeding-specific source notes that a dedicated breeding surface is necessary, and that something as simple as a small upturned plant pot is sufficient.

Feeding

No source checked for this guide gives detailed species-specific feeding behavior, but one breeding-focused data sheet recommends food that reaches the bottom third of the tank — sinking flakes and granules as a base diet, with live or frozen bloodworm and brine shrimp to bring fish into breeding condition. That matches the genus-standard approach used elsewhere on this site. See our general feeding guide.

Temperament & Compatibility

No species-specific temperament report was found in the sources checked for this guide. Treat it with the same caution recommended genus-wide elsewhere on this site: calm, unobtrusive tankmates, and care around other Apistogramma species given the genus's hybridization risk. See our social dynamics guide for general stocking guidance.

Breeding

Wapisana is presumed to be a cave spawner, consistent with every other species on this site, though no confirmed breeding report turned up in the sources checked for this guide. One breeding-focused data sheet suggests the standard approach for a group without an already-bonded pair: keep one male with several females in a dedicated tank and let a pair form on its own, provided with a simple breeding surface such as a small upturned plant pot. A separate general aquarium reference independently rates captive breeding of this species as very difficult — the two sources agree, in effect, that successful spawns are rare and essentially undocumented. Follow our general breeding guide for the standard conditioning-to-fry process until more species-specific detail exists.

Sourcing Notes

This is one of the harder species in this site's library to actually find. Cichlidae.com calls it "a very rare species," and the clearest documentation available is a single aquarium photograph rather than any established retail presence. If you do encounter wapisana offered for sale, confirm the seller can speak to its origin — misidentification risk runs high for any genuinely rare, rarely-photographed apisto — and see our general sourcing guide for broader guidance on vetting a seller's claims.

Final Thoughts

Wapisana sits at the genuinely obscure end of this site's library: a 2006 description from one small stream behind a bridge outside Boa Vista, named for the Wapishana people of that Roraima borderland, and still represented in the hobby by little more than a single photographed specimen. It shares a describer's pedigree with this site's own barlowi and baenschi, but none of their trade recognition. If you want a similarly rare, poorly-documented apisto with at least a confirmed hobby presence to compare against, see this site's own velifera or ortmanni.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where does Apistogramma wapisana come from, and what does its name mean?

The type locality is a small forest stream (igarapé) less than a kilometer behind the bridge on the road running east out of Boa Vista, the capital of Roraima state in northern Brazil. FishBase says the species epithet honors the Wapishana, also called Mawayana, an indigenous people of that Roraima region.

How big does Apistogramma wapisana get?

FishBase lists a maximum of 2.8cm (about 1.1 inches) standard length for males or unsexed fish, and 2.9cm (about 1.14 inches) for females — females recorded very slightly larger than males, a reversal of the male-larger pattern typical of this genus.

What water parameters does Apistogramma wapisana need?

FishBase gives a native-habitat pH of 5.5 to 6.0 in small, muddy, whitewater-influenced streams with heavy leaf litter, not the clear blackwater some other apistos on this site come from. A separate breeding-specific data sheet recommends slightly acidic, soft water — pH 6.0 to 6.5 and around 5°dH — for spawning attempts. See our general water parameters guide.

Is Apistogramma wapisana easy to breed?

No confirmed breeding reports were found in the sources checked for this guide, and one general aquarium reference separately rates captive breeding as very difficult. The standard approach without a bonded pair is one male with several females in a dedicated tank, given a cave-like breeding surface such as an upturned small plant pot.

Can I buy Apistogramma wapisana?

Treat sourcing as a serious obstacle. The species reference site Cichlidae.com describes it as "a very rare species," illustrated mainly by a single photo of a male in the aquarium of longtime Apistogramma authority Mike Wise rather than by established retail stock — closer to this site's own velifera or ortmanni in scarcity than to anything regularly imported.
