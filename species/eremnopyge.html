Apistogramma eremnopyge

Apistogramma eremnopyge

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Male to 2.8" (7 cm) SL, female to 2" (5 cm) SL — wild adults often run smaller
  • Tank: 24"×12" (~14 gal) minimum footprint for a pair; larger is better
  • pH: 4.0–6.0 — among the most acidic-water specialists in the genus
  • Temperature: 72–82°F (22–28°C)
  • Hardness: Very soft, 1–5 dGH, negligible KH
  • Temperament: Keep alone or with dither fish; avoid other Apistogramma species
  • Difficulty: Advanced (extreme softwater chemistry; mature tank required)

Overview

Apistogramma eremnopyge is a small Peruvian blackwater dwarf cichlid best known not for anything in its biology, but for how many different names it's been sold under. Collected from the Tapiche River near Requena in 2003, early imports were marketed as "Fresa" (Strawberry) and "Roterbart" or "Barbaroja" (Red Beard), both describing the red spotting on the face. The fish wasn't formally described until 2004, when Ready and Kullander named it Apistogramma eremnopyge from specimens collected by Oliver Lucanus in the Río Pintuyacu, a tributary of the Río Itaya in Peru's Loreto region — its DATZ reference code is A218. Today wholesale suppliers list it under yet another name, "Diamond." None of these are official common names; the species simply doesn't have one, which means the identical fish can appear on three different store labels depending on which import batch and era it came from. See our sourcing guide for why that matters when you're trying to confirm exactly what you're buying.

Chemistry-wise, this is a genus-extreme species. It comes from slow tributaries, backwaters, and leaf-litter creeks with very soft, clear-to-blackwater conditions, and its aquarium requirements track that closely — in the same demanding tier as baenschi and elizabethae rather than anything beginner-friendly.

Tank Setup

A single pair needs at minimum a 24 × 12 inch footprint (roughly 14 US gallons); this species does noticeably better with more room than the bare minimum.

  • Substrate: Soft sand.
  • Hardscape: Wood roots and branches arranged to create shaded pockets and caves — ceramic flowerpots or plastic pipe sections work just as well as natural caves.
  • Leaf litter: Genuinely useful here, not optional. Dried leaves or alder cones add cover, double as spawning sites, support the microbial growth that feeds fry, and release the tannins that mimic this species' natural water.
  • Planting and light: Keep lighting dim. Microsorum, Taxiphyllum (Java moss), and Cryptocoryne all tolerate the low light; floating Ceratopteris helps cut glare further.
  • Filtration: A gentle air-driven sponge filter is sufficient — this is a slow-water species, not a current-lover.
  • Tank maturity: Only add this species to a biologically established tank. It's notably sensitive to fluctuating organic waste in a way that punishes a rushed cycle.
  • Water chemistry: Plan on RO/DI water cut with a remineralizer or blended with tap to reach pH 4.0–6.0 and 1–5 dGH reliably — straight tap water almost never lands in this range on its own. Phosphoric acid can nudge pH down further if needed once hardness is already low.

Feeding

Carnivorous, feeding on benthic invertebrates in the wild. Offer live or frozen Artemia, daphnia, Moina, and bloodworm as a regular diet. Most individuals eventually accept dried foods too, generally taking to pellets more readily than flakes.

Temperament & Compatibility

Best kept alone, or with small, unobtrusive dither fish such as Nannostomus pencilfish — avoid combining it with other Apistogramma species. If you want to keep multiple pairs, a useful trick from breeders is housing each pair in its own tank where they can still see neighboring pairs; that visibility actually reduces aggression between pairs compared to cramming more than one pair into a single tank. Where space allows, one male with two to three times as many females in a larger setup is a more stable ratio than a single forced pair.

Mature males hold permanent territories and are aggressive toward other males nearby, though they'll tolerate several females moving through that territory.

Breeding

Conditioning

Condition on a steady diet of live and frozen foods before expecting spawns, and don't rush the tank — this species is specifically documented as sensitive to tanks that haven't fully matured biologically.

Spawning

A substrate spawner that lays 50 to 150 eggs in a crevice or cavity among the décor, usually at dawn or in darkness rather than in the open. After spawning, the male returns to patrolling his territory while the female takes over essentially all defense of the eggs and fry.

Fry Care

At 77°F (25°C), eggs hatch in about two days and fry become free-swimming five to six days after that. Start fry on microscopic foods before stepping up to newly hatched Artemia nauplii. The female typically drives the young off to fend for themselves after four to eight weeks, though they may linger at the edge of the male's territory a while longer. Keep temperatures at or below 81°F (27°C) during this period — breeders report that higher temperatures measurably reduce fry survival in this species.

Sourcing Notes

Because this fish has carried at least four different trade names across two decades of imports — Strawberry, Red Beard, Barbaroja, and now Diamond — the name on a store's tank label tells you very little on its own. Confirm the scientific name (Apistogramma eremnopyge, sometimes shown with its DATZ code A218) with the seller before buying, and ask about the import's origin if you want a known locality. This is a wholesale-trade fish more often than a retail-shelf one, so specialist importers and dedicated apisto breeders are a more reliable source than general retail — see our sourcing guide for the questions worth asking either way.

Final Thoughts

Eremnopyge rewards keepers who are past chasing an easy win: the water chemistry is genuinely demanding, on par with baenschi and elizabethae, and the inconsistent trade naming makes sourcing a known, healthy fish its own small research project before you even buy one. What you get in return is a compact, red-marked blackwater specialist that's been quietly circulating under different names for over twenty years without most keepers realizing it's the same fish. If you've already got the patience and RO/DI setup for baenschi or panduro, eremnopyge is a reasonable next extreme-softwater project.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Apistogramma eremnopyge sold under so many different names?

It's one fish that's been rebranded repeatedly since its 2003 import. Early stock was sold as "Fresa" (Strawberry) and "Roterbart"/"Barbaroja" (Red Beard), named for the red spots on its face. It wasn't formally described as Apistogramma eremnopyge until 2004, by Ready and Kullander. Today wholesalers list it as "Diamond." None of these are official common names — the species has none — so the same fish can show up under three or four different labels depending on when and where it was imported.

What water parameters does Apistogramma eremnopyge need?

Very soft and acidic: pH 4.0–6.0, 1–5 dGH with negligible carbonate hardness, and 72–82°F (22–28°C). This is genus-extreme water chemistry in the same range as baenschi and elizabethae, typically requiring RO/DI water to hit reliably from tap water.

How big does Apistogramma eremnopyge get?

Up to about 2.8 inches (7 cm) standard length for males and 2 inches (5 cm) for females in the aquarium, though professional breeders note wild-caught adults are often smaller, rarely exceeding 2 inches even as mature fish, and only reach the larger end of that range after extended captive growth.

Is Apistogramma eremnopyge a good community fish?

Not really. It does best kept alone with small dither fish like Nannostomus pencilfish, and shouldn't be mixed with other Apistogramma species. If you want to keep more than one pair, housing them in separate, visually adjacent tanks actually reduces aggression compared to one large shared tank.

How does Apistogramma eremnopyge breed?

It's a cave spawner, laying 50 to 150 eggs in a crevice or cavity, usually at dawn or in darkness. Eggs hatch in about two days at 77°F, and fry become free-swimming five to six days later. The female handles essentially all fry guarding while the male holds his territory nearby, and she typically drives the young off on their own after four to eight weeks.
