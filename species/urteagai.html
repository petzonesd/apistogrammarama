Apistogramma urteagai

Apistogramma urteagai

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: 1.6" (4.1cm SL) max for males/unsexed per FishBase — no separate female figure published
  • Tank: No species-specific minimum documented; kept in a 19-gallon flat breeder in a documented account
  • pH: 5.0–6.0 general (FishBase); 5.5–6.5 at the documented wild site; 6.0 in a documented home tank
  • Temperature: 72–84°F (22–29°C) general; 77°F wild, 78°F documented tank
  • Hardness: 6–10 dH general (FishBase); 12–22 ppm TDS wild vs. 45 ppm TDS documented tank — a real gap
  • Temperament: Cave spawner; described in one account as unusually calm and prolific
  • Difficulty: Intermediate (easy to breed by documented account, but wild/import-only sourcing)

Overview

Apistogramma urteagai is a modestly colored but, by one detailed hobbyist account, unusually easy-breeding dwarf cichlid from Peru's Madre de Dios basin. Sven O. Kullander formally described the species in 1986, naming it for Jorge Andrés Urteaga Cavero, a Peruvian biologist who co-led the expedition that collected the type material — the same naming pattern this site has already documented for species like meinkeni (named for hobby pioneer Hermann Meinken) and geisleri (named for ichthyologist Rolf Geisler). FishBase lists urteagai's common name as the Broadstriped dwarf cichlid and gives a maximum size of 4.1cm (1.6") standard length for males or unsexed fish, without publishing a separate female figure.

That "broadstriped" common name is worth a caveat: the most detailed documented keeper account of the species, a wild-collected group profiled by the Potomac Valley Aquarium Society (PVAS), doesn't describe obvious broad body stripes at all. Instead, author Don Kinyon describes courting males showing metallic teal through the body center and gill plates, set against an olive-drab back and a white belly, with some males carrying teal flecks on the anal fin and the first rays of the ventral fins. Females stay drab outside of spawning, then turn bright yellow with contrasting black markings while guarding eggs or fry — a dramatic, temporary color change rather than a permanent pattern. FishBase rates the species IUCN Least Concern, assessed April 25, 2014.

Natural Habitat

FishBase places urteagai's range in the Madre de Dios River basin of Peru, in small streams and swamps, with a general water-chemistry range of pH 5.0–6.0, 6–10 dH, and 72–84°F (22–29°C). A documented October 2017 PVAS collecting trip puts specific numbers on one site within that range: the Loboyoc marshlands near Puerto Maldonado, visited during the low-water season. The stream there ran shallow and muddy, with a stronger current down the center and calmer water along the edges, bordered by marshland. Water at that exact site measured pH 5.5–6.5, 12 to 22 ppm TDS, and about 77°F (25°C). Other fish collected alongside urteagai on the same trip included hatchetfish, brilliant blue tetras, and three Corydoras species, one of them the trade-named CW097.

That same October 2017 Peru expedition is documented in a separate PVAS report by the same author covering this site's own A. rubrolineata — collected from a different, specifically named creek ("Alegria") in the same broad Madre de Dios region of Peru, rather than from rubrolineata's formal Bolivian type locality. The two species aren't known to be close relatives, but both of their most detailed documented accounts trace back to the same hobbyist's reporting from the same general trip and region.

Tank Setup

  • Tank size: No species-specific minimum is documented in any source checked for this guide. The PVAS breeding account used a 19-gallon flat breeder-style tank to house a group of five males and six females. See our tank setup guide.
  • Substrate & décor: The documented tank used a thin layer of fine brown sand, with clay pots, driftwood, and Java fern providing cover and breeding territories. A single large sponge filter handled filtration.
  • Water chemistry: FishBase's general range is pH 5.0–6.0 and 6–10 dH. The documented home tank ran pH 6.0 using mostly rainwater, at about 45 ppm TDS — noticeably harder than the 12–22 ppm TDS measured at the wild collection site, a gap the author attributes in part to the tank setup omitting the wild site's mud rather than fully replicating it. See our water parameters guide.
  • Water quality: The documented account used twice-weekly partial water changes of about 15% for fry-rearing tanks, rising to three or four times weekly as fry grew. No separate adult-tank schedule is given beyond the general genus standard. See our water quality guide.

Feeding

The documented PVAS account describes an unfussy adult diet: daphnia, mosquito larvae, glass worms, live black and white worms, chopped earthworms, and flake foods, on which the females reportedly grew stout quickly. Fry were raised on newly-hatched brine shrimp, with decapsulated brine shrimp eggs or powdered micro foods used at times, fed until their bellies looked visibly bloated. See our general feeding guide for broader conditioning advice.

Temperament & Compatibility

The one detailed documented account available describes urteagai as calm enough to house a mixed group of five males and six females together in a single 19-gallon tank, with males patrolling territory and chasing off rivals during spawning rather than any account of serious aggression. No source checked for this guide documents specific tankmate recommendations beyond the general genus standard of avoiding aggressive or fin-nipping species; the author's own tank housed the Apistogramma alongside no other fish species in the breeding setup described.

Breeding

The best documented account of this species' breeding calls it one of the easiest and most prolific dwarf cichlids the author had kept — a genuinely distinctive claim worth taking at face value given how detailed the record is. In the PVAS account, spawning began within about a week of the wild-collected group entering their 19-gallon tank. Females laid roughly 50 maroon eggs apiece under clay pots, which hatched in about two days, with fry free-swimming roughly three days after that. Females fanned and relocated their broods and guarded them closely, while males patrolled the outer territory and chased off rivals without approaching the nests directly. At points during the account, up to four females held active broods simultaneously. The author managed the resulting crowding by moving larger fry to separate grow-out tanks, and estimated a total population of 150 to 200 fish within a few months of the original group's arrival. In the author's own words, urteagai are "some of the best parents I've found, and most definitely the most prolific," even while acknowledging the species "may not be the most colorful of the dwarf cichlids." Follow our general breeding guide for the standard conditioning-to-fry process.

Sourcing Notes

No source checked for this guide documents urteagai as a regular retail species; the most detailed account available describes a specific wild-collected group brought back from a documented Peru expedition rather than ongoing commercial-scale export. Budget for sourcing through specialist importers, breeders, or aquarium clubs rather than expecting to find it at general retail, and see our general sourcing guide for more on vetting specialist-import claims.

Final Thoughts

Urteagai is a quietly well-documented species for how rarely it's discussed: a single detailed hobbyist account covers its wild collection, captive setup, and breeding in unusual depth, and that account makes an unusually strong claim — that this is among the easiest and most prolific Apistogramma to breed, even if it isn't the most colorful. If you want to research the same broad Peru expedition and region alongside it, this site's own rubrolineata was documented by the same author from the same general October 2017 trip, though the two aren't known to be close relatives.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who was Apistogramma urteagai named after?

Jorge Andrés Urteaga Cavero, a Peruvian biologist who co-led the expedition that collected the type material. Sven O. Kullander formally described the species in 1986 from Peru's Madre de Dios River basin. FishBase lists its common name as the Broadstriped dwarf cichlid, though a detailed 2018 hobbyist account of a wild-collected group doesn't describe obvious broad stripes — the author instead describes courting males showing metallic teal through the body center and gill plates, over an olive-drab back and white belly, which is worth knowing going in if you're expecting a strongly striped fish.

Is Apistogramma urteagai easy to breed?

By documented account, unusually easy. A detailed December 2018 report from the Potomac Valley Aquarium Society (PVAS), describing a wild-collected group from an October 2017 Peru trip, calls urteagai "some of the best parents I've found, and most definitely the most prolific" of the dwarf cichlids the author had kept. In that account, spawning began within about a week of the fish entering a 19-gallon tank, with up to four females brooding roughly 50 eggs each under clay pots simultaneously, and the author estimated 150 to 200 total fish within a few months.

Where does Apistogramma urteagai come from, and what water does it need?

Peru's Madre de Dios River basin, in small streams and swamps. FishBase gives a general range of pH 5.0–6.0, 6–10 dH, and 72–84°F (22–29°C). A documented October 2017 collection trip measured the specific wild site — the Loboyoc marshlands near Puerto Maldonado, in low-water season — at pH 5.5–6.5, 12–22 ppm TDS, and about 77°F (25°C). The same keeper's home tank ran noticeably harder at 45 ppm TDS using mostly rainwater, at pH 6.0 and 78°F — a real gap the author attributes partly to the captive setup omitting the wild site's mud, not a full replica of the collection water.

How big does Apistogramma urteagai get?

FishBase lists a maximum of 4.1cm (1.6") standard length for males or unsexed fish and does not publish a separate female figure, so treat this as a small, typically-sized Apistogramma rather than expecting precise male-vs-female numbers. The only detailed documented growth data comes from fry rather than adults: a PVAS breeding account recorded about 3/8 inch (roughly 1cm) at three weeks and about 3/4 inch (roughly 2cm) at eight weeks.
