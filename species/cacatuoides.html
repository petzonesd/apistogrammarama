Red-nosed Apisto (Apistogramma cacatuoides)

By Pet Zone SD Team • September 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: 2–3 inches
  • Tank: 20 gallons long minimum
  • pH: 5.5–6.8
  • Temperature: 75–82°F
  • Hardness: Soft to slightly hard (2–8 dGH)
  • Temperament: Peaceful to semi-aggressive (males territorial)
  • Difficulty: Beginner to intermediate

Overview

Apistogramma cacatuoides is the gateway drug to dwarf cichlids. It's the species that launched a thousand hobbyists into the realm of breeding small cichlids, and for good reason: it's hardy, colorful, widely available, and forgiving of mistakes.

The red-nosed apisto earned its common name from the red blotch on the male's head (the nape), which becomes more vivid during breeding and stress. Males display elaborate fins and bold color patterns. Females are smaller, less colorful, but no less interesting — and they're the ones doing the heavy lifting during breeding.

This species is found throughout the Rio Ucayali drainage in Peru. Wild populations show regional variation in color and size, which we'll discuss in the Variants section.

Tank Setup

Tank Size & Footprint

Minimum: 20 gallons long (30" × 12" × 12"). Never less than this. They need horizontal space to establish territories.

Larger is always better. A 40-gallon breeder (36" × 18" × 18") allows you to house multiple pairs and fry without constant conflict. But 20-gallon longs work if you're patient about aggression and willing to remove fry promptly.

Substrate & Hardscape

Use a fine substrate — sand or small gravel. Apistos dig and sift constantly. This is not decoration; it's core behavior.

  • Sand: Best choice. Buy aquarium sand (not play sand). Allows them to forage naturally.
  • Small gravel: Works but less ideal.

Provide caves and hiding spots. Clay pots, driftwood, and dense plants all work. Males will claim a cave as territory; females will use them for spawning. A single male in a 20g needs at least 3–4 potential spawning sites.

Avoid bare tanks. An apisto in an open tank is a stressed apisto.

Plants

Plants are not optional — they're essential for fry survival and stress reduction. Easy plants work fine:

  • Microsword, chain sword, or other stem plants for planting
  • Java fern and Anubias (tie to driftwood, don't bury)
  • Floating plants for fry cover (Water sprite, frogbit)

Apistos will uproot plants. Use heavy pots or tie plants down.

Water Chemistry

Parameter Range Optimal
pH 5.5–6.8 6.0–6.5
Temperature 75–82°F 76–80°F
Hardness (dGH) 2–8 4–6
Nitrate <20 ppm <10 ppm

Why soft water matters: Cacatuoides come from soft water. They tolerate harder water but struggle to breed in it. If you're serious about breeding, invest in RO (reverse osmosis) and blend it with tap water, or use distilled water.

pH drop over time is normal. Driftwood and peat lower pH naturally. Once established, the tank will drift to your target pH. Test weekly until stable.

Filtration

Use a sponge filter or hang-on-back (HOB) with adjustable flow. Apistos dislike strong currents. A 40-micron sponge on a 5-10 GPH air pump is ideal for a 20g. Hang-on-backs should be set to low flow.

Frequent water changes are your real filter. Change 30–50% weekly. This removes waste, keeps nitrate down, and maintains pH stability better than any equipment.

Feeding

Apistos are carnivorous. In the wild, they eat small invertebrates. In the tank, they prefer live and frozen foods.

Daily Diet

  • Live foods (best): Micro worms, daphnia, small live shrimp, bloodworms
  • Frozen foods (good): Bloodworms, mysis shrimp, brine shrimp
  • Dry foods (supplement only): High-quality micro pellets or finely crushed pellets

Feed small portions 1–2 times per day. They have small mouths and stomachs. Overfeeding kills more apistos than underfeeding.

For breeding: Feed heavy with live foods 4 weeks before conditioning. This is non-negotiable for spawning success.

Temperament & Compatibility

Red-nosed apistos are peaceful with most fish — but not with each other. Males are highly territorial.

Male Behavior

A single male will claim a cave and defend it viciously. He'll harass females outside of breeding season. Two males in one tank means constant fighting and hiding.

The exception: Fry raised together may tolerate each other as adults, but this is rare. Assume one male per tank.

Females

Females are less aggressive. Three or more females can coexist in a large tank (40g+), but they'll still establish hierarchy. Expect hiding and chasing, especially during the female's post-spawning phase.

Compatible Tank Mates

  • Small tetras: Neons, cardinals, rummynose (30+ fish to avoid being eaten)
  • Corydoras catfish: Ideal — peacefully share the bottom, breed at the same time, enjoy similar water conditions
  • Small plecos: Otocinclus work; larger plecos may compete for caves
  • Shrimp: Amano and larger species coexist; crystals will be eaten as fry grow

Avoid

  • Other cichlids (dwarf or larger)
  • Large tetras or barbs that nip
  • Bottom-dwellers that compete for caves

Breeding

Conditioning

Breeding doesn't happen by accident. You must condition the pair 4 weeks before spawning.

  1. Feed heavy: Live daphnia and bloodworms, 2–3 times daily
  2. Frequent water changes: 50% daily to remove waste metabolites
  3. Temperature stability: Hold at 78–80°F (cooler than usual)
  4. Watch the female: Her belly will swell with eggs. When she looks like she'll burst, the pair is ready to spawn.

Spawning

The female will select a cave (usually a clay pot on its side). The male will hover outside, fanning her. Spawning happens at dusk — discrete, fast. You'll see 50–200 eggs stacked in the cave roof.

The female guards fiercely. The male guards the perimeter. This is normal. Don't panic.

Fry Care (The Hard Part)

  • Days 1–3: Eggs hatch, fry remain attached to the cave roof
  • Days 4–7: Fry become free-swimming. Feed microworms constantly (every 3–4 hours if possible)
  • Week 2+: Introduce newly hatched brine shrimp, crushed flakes, micro pellets

Fry need live foods to grow. Microworms are non-negotiable. Without them, survival rates plummet.

Tank size for fry: Raise in the breeding tank if it's 20g+. Crowding causes stunting and disease. Once they're 1 month old and 0.5 inches, you can move them to a larger nursery or separate them.

Variants & Regional Differences

Red-nosed apistos show enormous color variation based on origin. The hobby has several established lines:

Double Red (Super Red)

Both face and dorsal intensely red. Most popular in the hobby. Typically German or Czech captive-bred stock. Less hardy than wild lines, but more colorful.

Black-Stripe

Horizontal stripe running the lateral line, with red nape and dorsal. From certain river systems. More subdued coloring but hardy.

Orange (San Francisco)

Entire body has orange wash. Named for a line popularized by a U.S. breeder. Intermediate in hardiness.

Wild-Caught

Gray-green with red accents. Smaller average size. More difficult to acclimate but hardier once established. Rarer in the hobby now.

Why it matters: Captive-bred lines are easier for beginners. Wild lines breed true but are harder to keep. F1 fish (one wild parent) are in the middle.

Common Issues

Aggression / Hiding

Cause: Territorial stress, usually from multiple males or insufficient caves.

Fix: Ensure only one male per tank. Add more caves. Increase plant density.

Fading Color

Cause: Stress, poor diet, or water quality issues.

Fix: Test water (ammonia, nitrite, pH). Increase live food. Check nitrate (should be <10 ppm). Maintain 76–80°F.

Bloating / Pinecone Appearance

Cause: Dropsy, often from poor water quality.

Fix: Isolate and treat with salt or antibiotics. Prevention is better — maintain water quality obsessively.

Failed Spawns (Eggs Turn White)

Cause: Fungus, usually from poor conditioning or inadequate male fanning.

Fix: Condition longer next time. Ensure male is active and not stressed. If fungus appears, the batch is usually lost; remove it to prevent spread.

Fry Die-Off (Low Hatch Rates)

Cause: Inadequate live foods, poor water quality, or infertile male.

Fix: Microworms are essential. Start 2 weeks before expected spawn. Change 50% water daily. If no fry survive, the male may be infertile — try a different male.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Not all cacatuoides are equal. Poor genetics, inbreeding, and disease-laden farm stock are common.

  • Established breeders: Ask local clubs or online forums for recommendations. Breeders sell healthier fish and will answer questions.
  • Specialty aquarium stores: Pet Zone SD keeps quality cacatuoides when in stock. We source from reputable breeders and acclimate fish properly.
  • Avoid: Big-box pet stores and online sellers with fast shipping and no guarantees. Shipping stress kills weak fish.

Cost: Quality cacatuoides run $8–15 each. Cheap fish are cheap for a reason.

Final Thoughts

Red-nosed apistos are the entry point to a hobby within a hobby. Once you've bred them, you'll understand why dwarf cichlids captivate people for decades. The combination of color, personality, and breeding complexity is unmatched in small aquariums.

Start with a single pair, a 20-gallon long, soft water, live foods, and patience. Watch them interact, condition, and spawn. Raise fry if you can. You'll never look at "just pet fish" the same way again.
