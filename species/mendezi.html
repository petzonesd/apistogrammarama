Apistogramma mendezi

Apistogramma mendezi

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Male to 2.75" (7cm), female to 2" (5cm)
  • Tank: 24 gallons minimum, long footprint preferred
  • pH: 4.0–6.0 — among the most acidic in the genus
  • Temperature: 73–84°F (23–29°C)
  • Hardness: Very soft, 1–5 dGH
  • Temperament: Peaceful but territorial; males rough with females when conditioning
  • Difficulty: Advanced — extreme water chemistry, specialist sourcing, and a hard breed

Overview

Apistogramma mendezi is named after Francisco "Chico" Mendes, the Brazilian rubber tapper and rainforest-conservation activist murdered in 1988 — making it one of the only Apistogramma species named for a human-rights and environmental figure rather than a collector, scientist, or physical feature. Uwe Römer described the species in 1994 from blackwater habitats in the Rio Negro system in Amazonas State, Brazil, and chose the name specifically to honor Mendes, who organized rubber tappers against Amazon deforestation and was killed by a rancher shortly after his campaign began drawing international attention. (Römer used the Spanish spelling "mendezi" rather than the Portuguese "mendesi.")

Beyond the name, mendezi is a true blackwater specialist living among leaf litter along the shores of acidic forest streams and flooded margins — similar territory to baenschi and elizabethae on this site, and just as demanding of genuinely soft, acidic water. It remains uncommon in the hobby: it's imported only occasionally and mostly through specialist channels, so documentation is thinner than for widely kept species.

Color & Identification

Mendezi is notably polychromatic — males from the very same wild population can show visibly different color patterns, rather than one standard look that only varies by locality. One well-documented pattern has broad longitudinal stripes through the lower caudal fin and round spots across the upper half, with a distinct dark "belly stripe" running below the main lateral stripe; researchers suspect this patterning helps females tell same-species males apart from the similar-looking A. paucisquamis, which shares the same blackwater habitat. Males carry blue ventral and anal fins, a dorsal fin mixing red, brown, yellow, and blue, and a lyre-shaped (forked) tail with mottled dark markings. Females are plain gray-brown most of the time, but turn bright yellow with bold black markings when courting or guarding a brood — a dramatic, almost unrecognizable shift from their resting coloration.

Tank Setup

Give mendezi a mature, well-established tank rather than a freshly cycled one — like other blackwater specialists on this site, it handles unstable new-tank chemistry poorly. A 24-gallon long or similar footprint works well for a trio.

  • Substrate: Fine, soft sand — mendezi forages through leaf litter and fine substrate in the wild.
  • Hardscape: Driftwood, bogwood, and several caves or clay pots per female, since more than one female will each want her own refuge.
  • Leaf litter: Catappa or oak leaf litter is close to essential here, both for the tannins that help hold pH down and for cover that mimics its real blackwater habitat.
  • Filtration: Gentle flow from a sponge filter rather than a powerful hang-on-back — see our tank setup guide for why apistos generally prefer low current.
  • Water changes: Frequent and careful, since the acidity this species needs leaves less buffering room for mistakes than a higher-pH setup would.

Feeding

Omnivorous with a strong lean toward live and frozen foods: mosquito larvae, bloodworm, white worms, chopped earthworm, Daphnia, and brine shrimp all condition this species well for breeding. It will take a quality pellet or flake once settled, but live and frozen foods matter more here than for an easygoing species like cacatuoides — see our general feeding guide for the reasoning behind conditioning diets.

Temperament & Compatibility

Described as peaceful toward other species but genuinely territorial within its own, and males can be rough on females — reports describe a male keeping females cornered and underfed during conditioning badly enough that removing him was necessary before the females recovered and spawned. The safer approach is one male with several females and enough caves and visual breaks that each female can hold her own territory out of the male's constant sight. As with most apistos on this site, avoid housing it with other Apistogramma species, and stick to calm, small dither fish such as tetras, hatchetfish, or pencilfish.

Breeding

Mendezi is a standard cave spawner, but it's considered one of the harder Apistogramma species to breed successfully. The female lays roughly 40–60 eggs on the ceiling of a cave, flowerpot, or pipe, and the male fertilizes them. The water has to be genuinely soft and acidic for the eggs to develop — keepers who've spawned it report that eggs laid in water that isn't acidic enough develop fungus and fail to hatch, while lowering pH further (including, in one documented case, with dilute phosphoric acid) let later spawns from the same pair hatch successfully. Eggs that do take generally hatch in two to four days depending on temperature, and the female alone tends and guards both eggs and larvae in the classic Apistogramma pattern. Because males can be aggressive, some breeders remove the male once spawning is confirmed, particularly in a smaller tank. Fry start establishing territories of their own by around six weeks old and can be raised on infusoria and baby brine shrimp, moving on to chopped frozen foods as they grow — see our general breeding guide for why early fry-stage water stability is usually what makes or breaks a spawn like this one.

Sourcing Notes

Mendezi moves through specialist blackwater-cichlid and apisto importers only occasionally, and supply tends to run through the wholesale trade rather than general retail — expect to ask a dedicated apisto or South American cichlid specialist rather than finding it at a typical chain store. Because it's uncommon and polychromatic, don't assume a "mendezi" listing guarantees a matched color pattern or even a confirmed pair; buying a small group of juveniles and letting pairs sort themselves out, as covered on our general sourcing guide, is the more realistic path than hunting for a pre-paired adult male and female.

Final Thoughts

Mendezi rewards a keeper who wants genus-extreme water chemistry and a genuinely difficult breed, with a conservation story behind the name that's rare in this hobby — most Apistogramma are named for scientists, collectors, or a physical trait, not an activist murdered defending the very forests these fish come from. If you already keep baenschi or elizabethae and want a next challenge with real historical weight attached to it, mendezi is worth specifically seeking out.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Apistogramma mendezi named after Chico Mendes?

Uwe Römer, who formally described the species in 1994 from Brazil's Rio Negro system, named it for Francisco "Chico" Mendes — a rubber tapper, trade unionist, and rainforest-conservation activist who organized against Amazon deforestation and was murdered by a rancher on December 22, 1988, shortly after his campaign began drawing international attention. Römer used the Spanish spelling "mendezi" rather than the Portuguese "mendesi." It's one of the few Apistogramma names honoring a human-rights and environmental figure rather than a collector, scientist, or physical trait.

What water parameters does Apistogramma mendezi need?

Soft and strongly acidic blackwater: pH 4.0–6.0 and 73–84°F (23–29°C), with very low general hardness (roughly 1–5 dGH). It comes from leaf-litter-lined blackwater streams and flooded forest margins of the Rio Negro basin in Brazil, among the most acidic habitats in the genus. Breeding success in particular depends on getting the water soft and acidic — eggs from spawns in harder or less acidic water are prone to fungusing before they hatch.

How big does Apistogramma mendezi get?

Males typically reach around 2.5–2.75 inches (6–7cm) with longer, more colorful fins; females stay smaller, closer to 2 inches (5cm) and far plainer-looking outside of breeding. Sources vary somewhat on the exact ceiling, which is normal for a species that's still uncommon enough in the hobby that few keepers have raised large sample sizes to full size.

Is Apistogramma mendezi easy to breed?

No — it's considered one of the more difficult apistos to spawn successfully, and most keepers who've written it up call it rarely bred. Males can be rough with conditioning females, so a roomy breeding tank with sightline breaks helps. It's a standard cave spawner: the female lays 40–60 eggs on a cave ceiling and both eggs and newly hatched larvae need genuinely soft, acidic water to develop; eggs laid in water that isn't acidic enough tend to fungus before hatching.

Where can you buy Apistogramma mendezi?

It's imported only occasionally, mostly through specialist blackwater-cichlid and apisto importers rather than general retail, and supply skews toward the wholesale trade. Buy a small group of juveniles rather than expecting a guaranteed pair, since reliable sexed pairs are harder to come by for a species this uncommon — see our general sourcing guide for the same advice applied genus-wide.
