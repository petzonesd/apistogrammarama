Apistogramma elizabethae

Elizabeth's Dwarf Cichlid

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Male to 2.2" (5.5 cm), female to 1.6" (4 cm)
  • Tank: 20 gallons minimum, long footprint preferred
  • pH: 4.0–6.0 — true Rio Negro blackwater
  • Temperature: 73–84°F (23–29°C)
  • Hardness: Very soft, 1–3 dGH
  • Temperament: Semi-aggressive; males fiercely territorial toward other males
  • Difficulty: Advanced

Overview

Apistogramma elizabethae, known as Elizabeth's dwarf cichlid, is a true Rio Negro blackwater specialist whose males carry striking red and blue facial markings alongside dramatically elongated dorsal fin rays. It comes from the rio Uaupés and rio Içana tributaries of the upper rio Negro in Brazil, where it holds to slow-moving tributaries, backwaters, and leaf-litter creeks stained almost black with tannins. Sven Kullander described the species in 1980, naming it for Elizabeth, the wife of the noted ichthyologist and explorer Heiko Bleher.

Sexual dimorphism here is pronounced even by apisto standards. Males grow noticeably larger, with a reddish belly, blue-flecked facial markings, and showy extended dorsal rays used in both courtship and territorial display. Females run smaller and plainer — a muted grey-brown — outside of breeding, then flip to a striking, saturated yellow with bold black blotches the moment they're guarding a brood, at which point they also turn genuinely aggressive toward anything nearby.

Tank Setup

This is a species to set up for real blackwater, not water that's merely been softened and called close enough — it comes from some of the most tannin-stained, acidic habitat in the whole genus.

  • Substrate: Fine, dark sand mirrors the leaf-litter-floored creeks this species comes from and suits its benthic foraging habit.
  • Leaf litter: Not optional. A real layer of Indian almond, oak, or beech leaves pulls pH down toward the 4.0–6.0 range elizabethae actually needs, stains the water appropriately, and seeds the microfauna fry depend on.
  • Hardscape: Driftwood, root tangles, and dense low planting tolerant of soft, acidic water. Break sightlines with wood and plants — males are fiercely territorial toward other males and need places to disappear from one another.
  • Filtration: Gentle flow, matching the slow backwaters and tributaries it's found in rather than open river current.
  • Water source: San Diego tap water is nowhere near soft or acidic enough on its own. RO/DI water remineralized to target and cut with real leaf litter or a blackwater extract is the realistic path to parameters that hold.
  • Tankmates to avoid: Ornamental shrimp. Elizabethae is a genuine micropredator that hunts small shrimp and eats shrimp fry — this is not a species to pair with a breeding shrimp colony.

Feeding

Primarily carnivorous and best described as a micropredator in the wild, where it forages on small invertebrates. In the tank, most individuals accept quality dried pellets or flake that sink to the bottom third, where this species spends most of its time. Regular live or frozen foods — brine shrimp, daphnia, moina, and chironomid (bloodworm) larvae — noticeably improve color and are close to essential for bringing fish into breeding condition. Feed small amounts several times a day rather than one large meal.

Temperament & Compatibility

Rated semi-aggressive, with the sharpest edge aimed at its own species. Males are fiercely territorial toward other males, so keep strictly one male per tank unless the aquarium runs large and heavily broken up with wood and plants. A single male can be kept with one female or a small group of females. Small, fast dither fish such as Nannostomus pencilfish make good company and help the apistos feel secure enough to display their natural color and behavior.

Avoid mixing elizabethae with other Apistogramma species or color forms — overlapping territories raise both aggression and hybridization risk across the genus, and this species carries that risk within its own range too: a blue-headed form tied to the rio Içana and a deeper "super red" form tied to the Trovão tributary of the rio Uaupés should never be housed together, since mixing locality forms erases exactly the traits collectors value.

Breeding

Conditioning

Condition a bonded pair or a male-with-several-females group heavily on live and frozen foods, and don't rush the water chemistry — a tank that isn't genuinely soft and acidic rarely brings this species into breeding condition no matter how well it's fed.

Spawning

A substrate spawner that deposits eggs in a sheltered crevice, often an upturned coconut hut or small clay pot set up as a dedicated breeding surface. The female takes over essentially all post-spawning care and turns hyper-aggressive guarding the brood — in smaller tanks, the male may need to be removed to protect him from her during this window. He otherwise holds the surrounding territory from a distance.

Fry Care

Fry are highly sensitive to changes in water chemistry, more so than with hardier species on this site — stability matters as much as the numbers themselves. Start them on infusoria or a liquid fry food before moving on to newly hatched brine shrimp and microworms as they grow, and avoid any sudden parameter swings during this stage.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Ask any seller which locality or river system a fish traces to — the blue-headed Içana form and the "super red" Trovão form are both desirable in their own right, but only if they arrive true to type and aren't mixed with each other or with unrelated locality stock. Both wild imports and, less commonly, tank-bred fish show up in the trade. Visit our sourcing guide for more questions worth asking before you buy.

Final Thoughts

Elizabethae is one of this site's two most demanding species on water chemistry alone — only baenschi rivals it for how soft and acidic the tank genuinely needs to be. What you get in return is one of the most striking sexual-dimorphism stories in the genus, real Rio Negro blackwater authenticity, and a species that rewards keepers who already have soft-water husbandry dialed in. Skip it for a first apisto; consider it once you're ready to run true blackwater and keep ornamental shrimp well away from the tank.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Apistogramma elizabethae commonly called?

Elizabeth's dwarf cichlid, or sometimes just "elizabethae." Sven Kullander described the species in 1980, naming it for Elizabeth, the wife of ichthyologist Heiko Bleher.

What water chemistry does elizabethae need?

pH 4.0 to 6.0 and hardness of just 1 to 3 dGH — true Rio Negro blackwater. It's one of only two species on this site, alongside baenschi, that demands pH down near 4.0 to do well long term.

Can elizabethae be kept with ornamental shrimp?

No. It's a micropredator that actively hunts small ornamental shrimp and will eat shrimp fry on sight. Keep it out of any tank where breeding shrimp colonies are the priority.

Is Apistogramma elizabethae good for beginners?

No. Between the extreme water chemistry it needs and fry that are highly sensitive to any parameter swing, it's an advanced species best attempted after success with easier apistos like cacatuoides.

Do elizabethae color variants exist?

Yes. A blue-headed form is associated with the rio Içana drainage, and a deeper "super red" form is associated with the Trovão tributary of the rio Uaupés. The two should never be mixed in the same tank.

How aggressive is elizabethae?

Semi-aggressive. Males are fiercely territorial toward other males, so keep one male per tank unless the aquarium is large. It does fine alongside small, fast dither fish, but not with other Apistogramma species.
