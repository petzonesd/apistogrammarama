Apistogramma baenschi

Inca Dwarf Cichlid

By Pet Zone SD Team • October 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Male to 2.2" (5.5 cm), female to 1.6" (4 cm)
  • Tank: 20 gallons minimum, long footprint preferred
  • pH: 4.0–6.5 — among the most acidic in the genus
  • Temperature: 68–79°F (20–26°C)
  • Hardness: Very soft, 1–5 dGH
  • Temperament: Semi-aggressive; males and females both spar for dominance
  • Difficulty: Advanced

Overview

Apistogramma baenschi, better known in the trade as the Inca dwarf cichlid or by the strain name "Inca 50," demands some of the softest, most acidic water of any apisto commonly kept in the hobby — and rewards the effort with males that flush deep red-orange under a dramatically enlarged, sail-like dorsal fin. It comes from a narrow stretch of true blackwater habitat in the Shanushi and Paranapura drainages of the lower Río Huallaga, in the Amazonian lowlands of northern Peru near Yurimaguas. Described by Uwe Römer and Ingo Seidel and named for ichthyologist Jörg Baensch, it entered the hobby as a specialist's fish and has largely stayed one.

Males grow noticeably larger than females, with blue-flecked flanks, red-edged fins, and that signature oversized dorsal display used in both courtship and dominance contests. Females run smaller and duller outside of breeding, then brighten with yellow body tones when guarding a brood — a family resemblance shared across much of the genus, though baenschi's blackwater origin sets its water-chemistry demands apart from easier species like cacatuoides or borellii.

Tank Setup

This is not a species to approximate blackwater for — it needs the real thing, maintained consistently. A tank that's "softened a little" with no further attention will not hold this species long term.

  • Substrate: Fine sand for natural sifting and foraging behavior.
  • Leaf litter: Essential, not decorative. A real layer of Indian almond, oak, or beech leaves drives pH down toward the 4.0–6.5 range this species actually needs and seeds the microfauna fry rely on.
  • Hardscape: Driftwood, root tangles, and dense low planting that tolerates soft, acidic water (most Cryptocoryne and Bucephalandra species do well here). Break up sightlines — both sexes spar for dominance and need places to retreat.
  • Filtration: Gentle flow over strong turnover. This species comes from slow, shaded forest streams, not open water.
  • Water source: Tap water alone is rarely soft or acidic enough in most of San Diego. RO/DI water remineralized to target, cut with tannin-rich blackwater extract or real leaf litter, is the realistic path to stable parameters.

Feeding

A carnivorous micro-predator that forages on benthic invertebrates in the wild. In the tank it takes quality flake and micro-pellet readily, but condition and color improve noticeably with regular live or frozen foods — baby brine shrimp, daphnia, and bloodworms all work well. Feed smaller amounts two to three times daily rather than one large feeding; like most apistos, baenschi forages steadily through the day rather than gorging.

Temperament & Compatibility

Rated semi-aggressive rather than peaceful — both males and females actively spar for dominance within the species, which is a step up from the harem-friendly temperament of borellii or trifasciata. Keep strictly one male per tank unless the aquarium is large (4 feet or more), and expect some sparring between females even without a male present. Wild-caught individuals in particular do best alone or with small, fast dither fish rather than in a mixed community; captive-raised stock tends to settle into community tanks more easily.

Avoid mixing with other Apistogramma species in anything but a very large, heavily broken-up tank — the hybridization and territorial-conflict risk that applies across the genus applies here too, on top of baenschi's own within-species sparring.

Breeding

Conditioning

Condition on live and frozen foods for two to three weeks, and don't skip the water-chemistry groundwork — a tank that isn't genuinely soft and acidic will rarely bring this species into breeding condition no matter how well it's fed.

Spawning

A cave spawner, using a coconut hut, clay pot, or similar enclosed cavity. Eggs hatch quickly, typically within 36 to 72 hours. The female takes over guard duty and can turn genuinely aggressive toward anything approaching the cave, including the male at times, while he holds the wider territory.

Fry Care

Fry are small even by apisto standards. Start them on infusoria or a liquid fry food for the first several days before moving to newly hatched brine shrimp and microworms. Water quality matters more here than with hardier species — this is not a fish that shrugs off ammonia or nitrite spikes during the fry stage, and the soft water that suits the adults buffers poorly against swings if maintenance lapses.

Where to Get Quality Fish

Because "Inca" and "Inca 50" are strain names rather than separate species, ask the seller which locality or line a fish traces to if maintaining a pure strain matters to you — trade stock varies in how closely it holds to original wild coloration. Captive-raised fish adjust to aquarium conditions and community settings more readily than wild imports, and are the better starting point for most keepers. Final Thoughts

Baenschi sits alongside nijsseni and steindachneri as one of this site's strict blackwater specialists, but pushes the water chemistry further than either — it's tied only with elizabethae for the least forgiving species covered here on pH and hardness alone. That makes it a poor first apisto, but a genuinely rewarding one once you already have soft, acidic water chemistry under control and want a species whose color and temperament reward the extra effort.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Apistogramma baenschi good for beginners?

No. Baenschi needs some of the softest, most acidic water of any commonly kept apisto — pH down to 4.0 and hardness as low as 1 dGH — which makes it a poor fit until you've already run a stable blackwater tank successfully.

What does the trade name "Inca" or "Inca 50" mean?

Both are hobby names for Apistogramma baenschi tracing to its collection locality in the Shanushi and Paranapura drainages of the lower Río Huallaga in Amazonian Peru, near Yurimaguas. "Inca 50" in particular refers to a specific, well-regarded strain line, not a different species.

What water chemistry does baenschi need?

pH 4.0 to 6.5 and hardness of just 1 to 5 dGH — among the softest and most acidic requirements of any species in this genus kept in the hobby. Real blackwater conditions, not just a low pH number on a test strip, are what actually keep this species healthy long term.

Can baenschi live with other Apistogramma species?

It's best avoided, especially with wild-caught stock. Overlapping territories between Apistogramma species raise both aggression and hybridization risk, and baenschi's own males and females already spar for dominance within the species.

Is Apistogramma baenschi hard to breed?

Moderate once the water chemistry is genuinely dialed in. It's a cave spawner like most apistos, and eggs hatch quickly (36 to 72 hours), but a mature, truly soft, acidic blackwater tank is a precondition for consistent spawns, not a nice-to-have.
