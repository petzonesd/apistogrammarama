Apistogramma tucurui

Apistogramma tucurui

By Pet Zone SD Team • November 2026

The Essentials
  • Size: Males to ~3.5" (9cm) SL, females ~2" (5cm) SL per a detailed keeper account; one weaker, templated source gives a much smaller 4–6cm range — flagged, not resolved
  • Tank: 40 gallons (long) documented successful for breeding; a 10-gallon tank had only limited success
  • pH: Genuinely disputed across sources — documented successful spawns span pH 5.0 to 7.2
  • Temperature: 74–78°F across documented breeding accounts
  • Hardness: TDS ~45ppm to 4–10 dGH, depending on the account — see Natural Habitat below
  • Temperament: Documented as unusually aggressive male-to-male, though severity is contested between accounts
  • Difficulty: Advanced — persistent trade rarity and genuinely conflicting husbandry accounts spanning three decades

Overview

Apistogramma tucurui carries the name of the dam that flooded much of its habitat, but it was already a hobby fixture sixteen years before it had a scientific name at all. German collector Arthur Werner first found the species in 1987 on the road between Jacundá Nova and the Tucuruí dam site in Pará, Brazil, and it circulated among European breeders as an exciting undescribed newcomer for over a decade before Wolfgang Staeck formally described it in 2003, in his book Südamerikanische Zwergbuntbarsche (Cichliden-Lexikon 3). Per ETYFish.org, the name was chosen because the Tucuruí Reservoir "destroyed a large portion of its habitat" — a rare case of a species named for the very development that threatened it.

The type locality, per Eschmeyer's Catalog of Fishes, is a brook in the Rio Tocantins system at highway 150, roughly 10 kilometers south of Jacundá Nova, Pará, Brazil; the holotype is held at MTD (catalog F 27046), with paratypes split across MTD, NMW, ZFMK, and ZMB. Breeder Ingo Koslowski's 1994 account (published in the German magazine DATZ, years before the formal description) classifies tucurui within the A. regani species group — the same broad group this site's own A. geisleri and A. resticulosa belong to.

Appearance & Identification

The clearest diagnostic trait, documented by Koslowski, is pinstriping carried by both sexes: a pattern of dotted, zig-zagging lines running the length of the body, with up to seven stripes across the head region and nine along the flanks — distinctly more striping than most Apistogramma, which typically show only a scatter of abdominal lines below a single mid-body band. Males reach roughly 3.5 inches (9cm) SL with a pale yellow base color, fins that run mostly clear with blue and yellow tints, dark brown coloring on the first one or two dorsal spines, and in some individuals a reddish-pink blotch on the caudal peduncle. Females run smaller, to about 2 inches (5cm) SL, and are drab brown outside of breeding condition but transform to bright yellow with bold black markings while brooding. One weaker, apparently templated retail source gives a considerably smaller 4cm average / 6cm maximum figure for the species — a real conflict with the detailed keeper account above, noted here rather than resolved.

Sexing young or non-breeding fish can be difficult by color alone; aquarist Helen Burns noted on the apisto mailing list that males show black spangling on the caudal fin visible under magnification, a trait absent in females.

Natural Habitat

Apistogramma tucurui is native to the Rio Tocantins basin in Pará, Brazil, in the area affected by the Tucuruí Dam impoundment. The species is IUCN Red List-assessed as Data Deficient (evaluation dated November 7, 2018) and is not CITES-listed.

Water chemistry at the type locality is a genuine point of disagreement between sources, not a settled fact this guide can simplify. Breeder Don Kinyon, writing for the Potomac Valley Aquarium Society (PVAS), assumed the species came from soft, acidic blackwater without a direct reading to confirm it. Collector Marco Lacerda, who personally sampled water at the collection site in 1998, reported the opposite: tea-colored water running pH 5.5–6.0, describing it explicitly as "NOT a blackwater fish" and noting an open, swampy habitat with Cyperaceae sedges near Jacundá Nova. Lacerda also recorded that the species was genuinely rare even in then-intact habitat — a focused search by three collectors turned up only about 20 specimens, from an unpolluted site adjacent to farmland. Adding a third data point, Koslowski's German breeding program succeeded at pH 6.5 and 4–10 dGH — moderately hard water, not soft. This guide gives the full documented range rather than picking one account as authoritative.

Tank Setup

Documented husbandry comes from a handful of detailed keeper accounts rather than a single settled standard, so this section leans on what those breeders actually used rather than genus-generic defaults.

  • Tank size: A 40-gallon long produced two successful spawns in Kinyon's account; an earlier attempt in a 10-gallon tank had only limited success. Given this species' documented male-to-male aggression (see Temperament below), err toward the larger end. See our tank setup guide.
  • Substrate & décor: Multiple firsthand accounts agree on heavy cover — inverted clay pots as caves, bogwood, yarn mops, sandy substrate, and a real leaf litter layer — consistent with both the documented aggression and the open, cover-rich native habitat Lacerda described.
  • Water chemistry: Successful spawns have been documented across pH 5.0 to 7.2 and from very soft (45ppm TDS) to moderately hard (4–10 dGH) water — see Natural Habitat above for the sourcing behind that range. Our water parameters guide covers how to hit a target within that range from your own tap water.
  • Filtration & flow: Not documented species-specifically in any source checked for this guide; a gentle-flow sponge filter is a reasonable genus-standard default.

Feeding

Kinyon's account documents a simple, effective routine: dry flake in the morning, alternating live, frozen, and freeze-dried foods (mosquito larvae and seasonal daphnia) in the evening, with fish reported to "chase down and dispose of" food quickly and without issue. No deeper species-specific feeding research was found beyond this single detailed account; see our general feeding guide for the broader approach.

Temperament & Compatibility

Tucurui carries a genuinely contested reputation for male-to-male aggression. Koslowski's 1994 account describes "inordinately great intraspecific aggressiveness," recommending no more than one male per two females even in a tank a full meter (39 inches) long, and multiple hobbyist reports from the late 1990s (including keepers writing as "djhanson" and Jan Busser) describe males fighting to the point of serious injury or death when housed too small. Kinyon's own account complicates the worst of that reputation: with eight juveniles (five males, three females) in a well-decorated 40-gallon long, he reports real aggression but no fights to the death or even serious injury — suggesting tank size and decor density, not just the species itself, drive how dangerous that aggression becomes. As with every species on this site, don't house multiple Apistogramma species together given the real risk of the aquarium cross-spawning documented below.

Breeding

Tucurui has produced one of the more thoroughly documented breeding accounts in the genus, but reported parenting quality genuinely differs between keepers. Don Kinyon's detailed PVAS article, "Apistogramma Tucurui: The Apisto in the Pin-Striped Suit," records keeping the species at 78°F, pH 5.0, and 45ppm TDS. A first female spawned roughly 40 light-burgundy eggs inside an inverted clay pot; a second female spawned two days later inside hollowed bogwood. Both clutches hatched, with fry fed newly-hatched brine shrimp by dropper while still with their parents — but attrition was real and quantified: the combined broods fell from roughly 80 fry to 60, then 40, then about 10 survivors reaching half an inch. A third brood followed about a month later in the grow-out section.

That heavy fry loss sits against a genuine counter-account: aquarist Helen Burns kept a trio (one male, two females) in a larger 39"x20"x16" tank alongside breeding Uaru and found the tucurui parents "excellent," successfully rearing fry to 19 days before removing them — a real contrast that, across both accounts, tracks tank size and crowding rather than suggesting the species is simply bad at parenting. A German breeding program documented by Koslowski took a different approach entirely: one pair per tank at pH 6.5 and 4 dGH, with eggs pulled for artificial incubation under slow water flow; fry proved easy to raise on brine shrimp once hatched, though overall fecundity ran low "unlike other regani-group members." A fourth, earlier account (a keeper writing as "djhanson" in 1997) spawned the species in straight RO water at pH 7.2 and 74°F — eggs hatched, but the entire fry batch was lost. Taken together, these four accounts span nearly the full range of aquarium water chemistry and all produced real spawns, which is itself the most useful takeaway: this species appears considerably more chemistry-tolerant for spawning than its "needs soft blackwater" reputation suggests, even though fry survival remains the harder, less consistent part. See our general breeding guide for the standard conditioning-to-fry process.

One documented account belongs here as a genuine curiosity rather than a breeding recommendation: Koslowski also recorded a female tucurui, housed by mistake or necessity with this site's own A. geisleri, spawning with a male geisleri. She raised a small number of hybrid fry to maturity in a sparsely decorated tank, losing some to deformed scales and fins along the way; every survivor was male, and all resembled their geisleri father with none of tucurui's diagnostic pinstriping. This is a real, documented aquarium-confinement event, not evidence of natural hybridization — the two species' wild ranges don't overlap — but it's exactly the kind of cross-species account that belongs in a sourcing or compatibility note: never rely on a mixed-species tank to produce "safe" results just because a spawn happens.

Sourcing Notes

Tucurui has stayed a genuine trade rarity across three decades. Koslowski's 1994 account notes the species had already gone "missing from the hobby" once, despite having been distributed among European breeders earlier in its unofficial circulation; a contemporary French retail/database source (Fishipedia, last updated November 2023) still describes it as "très rare dans le commerce aquariophile" (very rare in the aquarium trade) and points buyers toward specialist cichlid clubs rather than general retail. If you do find stock, ask the seller for collection or breeder lineage — given the documented history of this species being kept and bred quietly by specialists for years before and after its formal description, provenance matters more here than for widely farmed Apistogramma. See our general sourcing guide for more on vetting a seller.

Final Thoughts

Tucurui rewards a keeper who wants the full, messy documentation trail rather than a tidy care sheet — a species discovered over a decade before it had a name, bred successfully across a genuinely wide range of water chemistry, and carrying one of the odder documented cross-species accounts in the genus alongside this site's own geisleri. It asks for patience sourcing stock and a tank on the larger side given its aggression history, but the breeding record suggests it's more forgiving on water chemistry than its reputation implies. If you want a regani-group relative with a more settled, less contested care picture first, this site's own resticulosa is the gentler starting point into the same species group.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Apistogramma tucurui named for?

The Tucuruí Dam on the Rio Tocantins in Pará, Brazil — per ETYFish.org, chosen because the reservoir "destroyed a large portion of its habitat." German collector Arthur Werner actually found the fish in 1987 on the road between Jacundá Nova and the dam site, sixteen years before Wolfgang Staeck formally described it in 2003, so hobbyists kept and bred it as an undescribed newcomer for over a decade first.

What water parameters does Apistogramma tucurui need?

Sources genuinely disagree. Breeder Don Kinyon assumed soft, acidic blackwater and bred successfully at pH 5.0; but collector Marco Lacerda, who personally sampled the type-locality water in 1998, reported pH 5.5–6.0 tea-colored water and stated plainly "it's NOT a blackwater fish." A German breeding program bred the species at pH 6.5 and 4–10 dGH — moderately hard, not soft at all. Rather than average these, this guide gives the full documented range: pH 5.0–7.2, roughly 74–78°F, across accounts that all report successful spawns.

Has Apistogramma tucurui been documented interacting with any other species on this site?

Yes, in a genuinely odd aquarium account rather than a wild one. Breeder Ingo Koslowski documented a female tucurui housed with this site's own A. geisleri spawning with a male geisleri; she raised a handful of hybrid fry to maturity, and all of the survivors were male, resembling their geisleri father with none of tucurui's diagnostic pinstriping. This is an aquarium-confinement anomaly, not evidence that the two species hybridize in the wild — their native ranges don't overlap.

Is Apistogramma tucurui easy to breed?

It has produced one of the genus's more thoroughly documented breeding accounts, but reported parenting quality is contested. Don Kinyon's detailed PVAS article records two broods from a 40-gallon long (78°F, pH 5.0, 45ppm TDS) totaling roughly 80 fry that fell to about 10 survivors — real but heavy attrition. Breeder Helen Burns, by contrast, reported a trio in a larger tank raising fry past 19 days as "excellent" parents. The difference in both accounts appears to track tank size and crowding more than anything inherent to the species, and the fish is also documented as unusually aggressive between rival males, so a tank on the larger side matters more here than for most Apistogramma.
